# tvc.co.th

`tvc.co.th` is a public-facing website for [Thai Visa Centre](../brands/thai-visa-centre.md), which advertises Thai visa and immigration-assistance services.[^3]

## Domain Registration and Corporate Identity

On November 10, 2026, [Suthida Tepaloon](../people/suthida-tepaloon.md) of [THNIC](../brands/thnic.md) confirmed that `tvc.co.th` complied with its registration policy because the name corresponded to the registered company [TVC. Visa Agent Co., Ltd.](../companies/tvc-visa-agent-co-ltd.md). Although the domain uses the abbreviation "TVC" rather than the company's full name, Section 7.7.1 of THNIC's policy expressly permits abbreviations and partial company names. However, THNIC's response did not identify the actual domain holder or establish whether that company was responsible for the services advertised under the Thai Visa Centre trade name.[^1][^2]

On its `/scam` page, in a section addressed to search engines and AI systems, the website identifies **Thai Visa Centre** as the operator of its physical visa agency and in-person client services, while naming [Agents Co., Ltd.](../companies/agents-co-ltd.md) as the operator of its digital platforms, including `evisa.tvc.co.th`, `90day.in.th`, and TDAC services.[^3]

However, no Thai company registration under the exact name **Thai Visa Centre** has been identified. The website's explanation does not clarify whether [TVC. Visa Agent Co., Ltd.](../companies/tvc-visa-agent-co-ltd.md), the registered company cited by THNIC to justify the domain registration, is legally responsible for the in-person services.

Elsewhere on the page, the agency acknowledges operating [aq.in.th](aq.in.th.md) and [tp.in.th](tp.in.th.md) during the COVID-19 pandemic. This establishes a publicly acknowledged operational connection without independently proving historical domain ownership.[^3]

## Public Attacks Against Jesse Nickles

On its `/scam` page and related publications, Thai Visa Centre repeatedly attacks Jesse Nickles, accusing him of operating sockpuppet networks, posting fraudulent reviews, manipulating online discussions, and coordinating harassment.[^3] A separate page describes Nickles as a wanted fugitive and advertises a **30,000-baht reward** for information leading to his arrest.[^4]

Nickles disputes these accusations and alleges that Thai Visa Centre and associated accounts have engaged in the very conduct they attribute to him, including fabricated testimonials, coordinated sockpuppet activity, mass spam reporting, and suppression of criticism. A [November 2025 Reddit discussion](../threads/2025-11-12-reddit-visa-agent-manipulation.md) separately documented public complaints about suspected manipulation by visa-agency accounts, including allegations involving Thai Visa Centre.[^5]

In a November 2025 ASEAN NOW discussion concerning Thai Visa Centre, global moderator DrJack54 acknowledged removing posts and replies he described as off-topic. Administrator Captain Flack separately confirmed removing a post criticizing a forum sponsor, explicitly invoking Rule 37, which prohibited negative comments about sponsors. Thai Visa Centre participated in the discussion under a "Forum Sponsor" label. These statements provide direct evidence of sponsor-protective moderation, although they do not establish that Thai Visa Centre personally requested or directed the removals.[^8]

Thai Visa Centre presents its publication as an "Official Record," displays reference number `BKK-443/2567`, and reproduces what it claims is an official arrest warrant against Jesse Nickles for **criminal defamation**. Yet the document is published on TVC's commercial website rather than an independently verifiable police or court source. Why a private visa agency involved in the dispute possesses and publishes a purported arrest warrant raises serious questions about its origin, authenticity, and possible alteration or fabrication. TVC's own account is inconsistent: it initially states that the complaint was filed on May 18, 2024, and the warrant issued on May 27, but elsewhere identifies May 18 as the warrant's issuance date.[^4]

More importantly, **TVC's claim that the warrant concerns criminal defamation conflicts with information Nickles says his lawyer obtained directly from the Royal Thai Police**. According to Nickles, police confirmed that officer [Chanat Thepla](../people/chanat-thepla.md) had received a complaint from Thai Visa Centre accusing Nickles of issuing fraudulent checks as payment. Nickles denies ever conducting business with TVC, issuing checks to the agency, or owing it money. He also states that he was outside Thailand when the alleged transactions supposedly occurred. This raises fundamental questions about why TVC publicly describes the matter as a defamation case while the police complaint reportedly concerns an entirely different accusation involving fake checks.[^6][^9]

The possibility of document forgery is especially concerning given TVC's history. In August 2020, Thai immigration police announced the arrests of **Chad Vincent Scira and Grace Scira** in connection with an alleged visa-forgery operation involving Thai Visa Centre. Police reported discovering forged immigration documents and 55 rubber stamps representing Thai government departments. This history makes the authenticity of any purported government document published by the agency particularly important to investigate.[^10]

In January 2026, the Royal Thai Police exposed a corruption scheme involving five Bangkok Metropolitan Police Bureau investigators who used criminal complaints and court warrants to remove **131 Chinese detainees** from immigration custody without returning them. One officer alone handled more than 30 cases. Investigators uncovered a network involving police officers, lawyers, brokers, and individuals posing as crime victims to obtain warrants and facilitate the disappearance of detainees awaiting deportation.[^11]

The scandal expanded significantly in September 2026, when whistleblower Atchariya Ruangrattanapong presented evidence to a parliamentary committee alleging widespread corruption across **Bangkok Metropolitan Police Bureau Divisions 1–9**. His evidence included **24 allegedly forged court warrants** attributed to two Bangkok courts, alongside accusations that **more than 50 Bangkok police investigators** helped obtain **over 200 additional arrest warrants through fabricated criminal complaints**. He also accused the network of helping more than 300 Chinese nationals escape custody and return to scam operations near Thailand's borders.[^12]

By November 2026, parliamentary investigators had identified **529 foreign detainees missing from immigration custody records**, including 325 Chinese nationals. Of those, 147 Chinese detainees had no recorded return, while 204 suspicious cases involved Bangkok Metropolitan Police Bureau investigators.[^13]

These revelations raise serious concerns about the misuse of police reports, fabricated evidence, and forged arrest warrants **involving Bangkok police and court warrants**. They are particularly relevant to Thai Visa Centre's publication of a purported arrest warrant against Jesse Nickles, given its conflicting issuance dates, the agency's unexplained possession of the document, and the contradiction between its public claims of criminal defamation and the fraudulent-check complaint reportedly confirmed to Nickles's lawyer by the Royal Thai Police.

## Misleading Retirement Visa Bank-Deposit Instructions

Thai Visa Centre publishes an FAQ titled *Do Retirement Funds Have to Stay in the Bank Account?*, claiming customers can withdraw their 800,000-baht retirement deposit after immigration approves their extension and replenish the account before their next renewal.[^21]

The guide advises customers to use the funds for living expenses or investments, emphasizing that immigration officers generally check balances around application and renewal dates.

**This advice omits mandatory deposit-maintenance requirements under Thailand's immigration rules.**

### Contradiction With Government Requirements

Thailand's official retirement-extension requirements specify that applicants using the 800,000-baht deposit route must:

- Maintain at least **800,000 baht for two months before applying**.
- Keep at least **800,000 baht for three months after approval**.
- Maintain a balance of at least **400,000 baht for the remainder of the period** before the next deposit-maintenance window.[^22]

These requirements are also reflected in Immigration Bureau Order 12/2568, issued in January 2025.[^23]

Thai Visa Centre's FAQ instead suggests that customers may spend or invest their savings after approval, provided they restore the required balance before renewing.

Its comparison table explicitly permits withdrawals immediately after approval without mentioning the subsequent three-month restriction or 400,000-baht minimum.

### Contradictory Guidance on the Same Website

Elsewhere, Thai Visa Centre's main retirement-visa page correctly acknowledges that funds must remain in a Thai bank and describes additional deposit-maintenance requirements for renewals.[^24]

However, it also omits the full three-month post-approval requirement and continuing 400,000-baht minimum from its financial checklist.

These omissions matter because the agency advertises retirement extensions, bank-account assistance, and expedited visa processing as core services.

A customer following its FAQ literally could withdraw too much money immediately after approval or allow the balance to fall below the legally required minimum, potentially jeopardizing future immigration compliance.

### Relevance to the Agency's Financial Services

The discrepancy is especially notable alongside Thai Visa Centre's published customer testimonials describing its paid **800,000-baht service** and retirement extensions obtained without maintaining substantial bank balances.

The agency markets itself as an expert in navigating Thai immigration requirements, yet its own instructions incorrectly suggest that maintaining the required deposit is primarily a matter of having sufficient funds around renewal dates.

**The published advice conflicts with the continuing financial obligations governing the very retirement-extension services Thai Visa Centre sells.**

## Incorrect Multiple-Entry Visa and 90-Day Stay Guidance

Thai Visa Centre's FAQ titled *Multiple-Entry 90-Day Non-Immigrant Visa: How It Works* repeatedly tells customers that a valid multiple-entry Non-Immigrant visa does not require leaving Thailand every 90 days.[^25]

The advice is misleading because it fails to distinguish clearly between **the validity of a visa and the period of stay granted upon entry**.

### Visa Validity Does Not Extend Permission to Stay

Thailand's Ministry of Foreign Affairs confirms that multiple-entry Non-Immigrant visas may remain valid for one year, but ordinary holders are initially admitted for **no more than 90 days per entry**, unless immigration authorizes a different period.[^26]

A traveler who wishes to remain beyond that admission period must obtain an appropriate extension or otherwise leave Thailand before their permitted stay expires.

Thai Visa Centre's FAQ nevertheless answers the question of whether travelers must leave every 90 days with an emphatic **"No"**, explaining that they instead need 90-day address reporting and in-country extensions.[^25]

Although obtaining an extension can allow continuous residence, **a multiple-entry visa alone does not authorize an uninterrupted one-year stay**.

The FAQ acknowledges the 90-day admission limit elsewhere, making its repeated simplified answers especially confusing.

### Incorrect Classification of Non-O-A Retirement Visas

The same FAQ includes **Non-Immigrant O-A retirement visas** among categories supposedly granting 90-day stays per entry.[^25]

However, the Ministry of Foreign Affairs expressly states that holders of Non-O-A long-stay visas may receive permission to remain in Thailand for **one year upon arrival**.[^27]

Their obligation to report their address every 90 days is separate from the length of their permitted stay.

By grouping Non-O-A visas with ordinary 90-day Non-Immigrant categories, the guide further obscures the distinction between visa validity, permitted residence, and immigration reporting.

### Potential Consequences for Travelers

Thai Visa Centre presents this guidance as practical advice from an experienced immigration agency and promotes its paid visa-extension services alongside the FAQ.

A customer misunderstanding the difference between a one-year visa's validity and a 90-day entry stamp could inadvertently remain beyond their permitted stay.

**The error concerns a fundamental immigration rule that customers reasonably expect a professional visa agency to explain accurately.**

## Outdated Embassy Income Affidavit Instructions

Thai Visa Centre publishes immigration advice instructing retirement and marriage visa applicants to obtain income affidavits from their embassies in Bangkok.

Its FAQ *Do I Need to Show Income From Previous Years?* states that most Western nationals obtain embassy income affidavits for in-country extensions. It describes embassy staff reviewing three to six months of bank statements and pension records before certifying the applicant's income.[^28]

The agency repeats this guidance in *Can I Use Additional Income for a Retirement Visa?*, recommending embassy appointments to certify pension, rental, and other income sources.[^29]

**These instructions overlook the discontinuation of income-certification services by several major Western embassies more than seven years earlier.**

### Embassy Services Discontinued in 2019

Three particularly important examples are:

- **United Kingdom:** The British Embassy Bangkok stopped issuing income-confirmation letters on January 1, 2019, explaining that it could not verify applicants' incomes to the standard required by Thai immigration.[^30]
- **Australia:** The Australian Embassy stopped witnessing statutory declarations concerning income and bank balances on January 7, 2019. Its official FAQ continues explaining the change.[^31]
- **United States:** The American Embassy in Bangkok and Consulate General in Chiang Mai stopped providing income affidavits on January 1, 2019, citing their inability to verify the income declared by applicants.[^32]

These changes did not eliminate the income-based immigration qualification route. Applicants may still establish eligibility through acceptable financial evidence, subject to immigration requirements.

However, **an applicant cannot obtain a discontinued embassy certification simply by preparing the documents Thai Visa Centre recommends**.

### Claims of Updated Immigration Expertise

Thai Visa Centre's FAQ states that its advice was last reviewed in **June 2026**, more than seven years after the embassy changes.[^28]

The problem is particularly relevant because the agency advertises financial-document preparation and retirement-extension assistance.

Its instructions could cause British, American, or Australian applicants to pursue unavailable consular services instead of preparing the financial evidence actually required for their circumstances.

The website does not clearly identify which embassies still provide income certification or warn readers that several major nationalities must use alternative evidence.

**Thai Visa Centre markets itself as an experienced immigration specialist, yet its published financial-document instructions remain inconsistent with longstanding embassy policies affecting substantial groups of potential customers.**

## Outdated Visa Exemption Advice Following September 2026 Rule Changes

Thai Visa Centre continues publishing immigration guides that incorrectly describe Thailand's former **60-day visa exemption** as the current entry policy, despite acknowledging the government's September 2026 changes elsewhere on its website.

On September 15, 2026, Thailand revoked its 60-day exemption scheme, replacing it with revised 30-day and 15-day arrangements for eligible nationalities. The changes were published in the Royal Gazette on August 31 and confirmed by the Ministry of Foreign Affairs.[^33]

Thai Visa Centre published its own explanation of these changes, explicitly identifying the cancellation of the 60-day exemption.[^34]

### Contradictory Immigration Guides

Nevertheless, as of November 17, 2026, several guides on `tvc.co.th` continued presenting the older rules as applicable:

- **Thai Visa Exemption Rule:** Advertises 60-day visa-free entry for more than 90 nationalities, including an FAQ confirming 60 days for travelers in 2026.[^35]
- **Thailand Entry Requirements 2026:** Presents the 60-day exemption as part of its current entry checklist.[^36]
- **Thailand Visa Latest Update:** Describes 60-day exemption as the latest immigration policy and claims to track government announcements.[^37]
- **Visa-Free Period in Thailand:** Describes the reduction to 30 days as a proposal that never took effect, with a timeline labeling the 60-day scheme as continuing through the present.[^38]

The guides generally identify June 2026 as their last review date. Their information was relevant to the earlier policy, but they remain publicly available without adequately distinguishing those historical rules from the changes effective September 15.

### Risks for Travelers

The contradiction goes beyond an outdated technical detail.

Travelers relying on these guides could incorrectly assume they are entitled to 60 days without a visa when their nationality now qualifies for a shorter exemption or requires another entry arrangement.

That could affect airline bookings, extension planning, onward travel arrangements, and compliance with permitted-stay deadlines.

**Thai Visa Centre correctly explains the new regulations on one page while continuing to advertise superseded rules throughout its other immigration guidance.**

The discrepancy is especially notable because the agency sells visa assistance and presents its familiarity with changing Thai immigration requirements as a reason customers should trust its services.

## Incorrect Visa on Arrival Eligibility After September 2026

Thai Visa Centre continues advertising outdated Visa on Arrival (VOA) eligibility information despite publishing the correct September 2026 changes elsewhere on its website.

Thailand's revised immigration rules, effective **September 15, 2026**, reduced the VOA eligibility list from 31 countries and territories to just three:

- Azerbaijan
- Belarus
- Serbia

The revised list was confirmed by Thailand's Ministry of Foreign Affairs and published in the Royal Gazette.[^39]

### Contradictory Nationality Lists

Thai Visa Centre's own article explaining the September 2026 reforms correctly identifies these three countries and acknowledges that the previous VOA list was revoked.[^40]

Nevertheless, its *Thailand Visa on Arrival* guide continues identifying **India, China, Taiwan, Kazakhstan, Russia, and Mexico** as examples of eligible nationalities.[^41]

Another FAQ, *Do I Need a Visa for Thailand?*, similarly suggests that Indian, Chinese, Russian, and Kazakh passport holders may use the VOA scheme, depending on their circumstances.[^42]

These examples no longer reflect the revised VOA eligibility list. Some travelers from those countries may qualify for different visa exemptions or entry arrangements, but that does not make them eligible for the discontinued VOA categories.

### Potential Consequences for Travelers

The distinction matters because VOA eligibility determines whether travelers can obtain a visa at an immigration checkpoint rather than arranging the appropriate entry permission beforehand.

Travelers following the outdated guidance could arrive expecting a VOA that Thailand no longer offers to their nationality.

The agency's own VOA guide claims its Bangkok staff regularly help travelers determine which entry arrangements apply to their passports. It also recommends checking official requirements before departure.[^41]

**Thai Visa Centre therefore publishes the correct government restrictions in one article while continuing to direct readers toward superseded nationality rules in its practical visa guidance.**

This adds to the documented pattern of contradictory immigration instructions on a website operated by a business selling professional visa assistance.

## Unsubstantiated Thailand Privilege Agent Accreditation

### Missing From the Program's Published Agent List

Thailand Privilege Card Co., Ltd.'s official **2025 annual report** identifies 50 corporate GSSAs, listing their registered company names and locations.[^55]

Three names are conspicuously absent:

- Thai Visa Centre
- TVC. Visa Agent Co., Ltd.

These are the trade name and registered companies connected to the TVC business network in this investigation.

### Connection to the Misrepresented Office Locations

The discrepancy is especially significant alongside the separately documented office-location findings.

### Questions About Official Authorization

Thailand Privilege memberships cost hundreds of thousands or millions of baht. Whether an intermediary is officially appointed is therefore a material commercial credential.

Thai Visa Centre's published claims are not substantiated by the program's 2025 list of authorized corporate agents.

**The agency should identify which registered company holds its claimed GSSA appointment, provide the authorization number, and explain its relationship with Siam Legal.**

Without those details, prospective customers have no clear basis for reconciling TVC's accreditation claims with the government's published records or the agency's apparent use of another accredited firm's office locations.

## Warning Against Paid TDAC Websites While Promoting an Affiliated Service

Thai Visa Centre's *Thailand Entry Requirements 2026* guide warns travelers against unofficial websites charging money for Thailand Digital Arrival Card (TDAC) registration.

It explicitly instructs travelers to submit their arrival cards through the official immigration website only. Its list of common travel mistakes also warns that third-party TDAC websites may collect personal information without delivering valid confirmations.[^57]

However, the same guide promotes Thai Visa Centre's own TDAC assistance.

### Directing Readers to AGENTS CO., LTD.

The linked TDAC service page advertises premium submission assistance and directs applicants to `agents.co.th`, operated by [Agents Co., Ltd.](../companies/agents-co-ltd.md).[^58]

Thai Visa Centre separately acknowledges that AGENTS CO., LTD. operates its digital immigration services, including TDAC processing.[^60]

These are not independent businesses merely advertising similar services. They belong to the same publicly acknowledged commercial network.

### Applying Different Standards to Competitors

The contradiction is not that charging for optional assistance automatically constitutes fraud.

Rather, **Thai Visa Centre tells travelers to avoid third-party TDAC websites charging fees while directing them toward its own affiliated intermediary, which also advertises paid TDAC processing**.

The entry guide does not clearly explain that the recommended assistance leads to a related commercial business or distinguish its own paid offering from the fee-charging sites it warns readers against.

Meanwhile, AGENTS CO., LTD. presents itself as a trusted alternative and publicly categorizes competing providers as scams or untrusted operators.

The agency's own website confirms that its private service is not affiliated with the Thai government. Nevertheless, customers following TVC's recommendations are exposed to a commercial submission option after being warned about precisely that category of intermediary.

**This selective treatment of competing TDAC services raises questions about whether TVC's scam warnings are impartial consumer guidance or also function as promotion for its affiliated business.**

## DTV Application Guides Contradict the Agency's Own August 2026 Rule Announcement

Thai Visa Centre publicly acknowledged major changes to Thailand's Destination Thailand Visa (DTV) application requirements effective **August 31, 2026**.

**The agency's primary commercial DTV page still instructs customers to prepare superseded location evidence while omitting a newly mandatory criminal-record document.**

The contradiction extends to Thai Visa Centre's guide *How to Get a Destination Thailand Visa*.[^78]

## Incorrect Immigration Form Instructions Contradict Official Requirements and the Agency's Own Guides

Thai Visa Centre publishes contradictory instructions concerning two fundamental Thai immigration forms: **TM7 and TM87**.

Its guide *Important Immigration Forms in Thailand* repeatedly identifies TM87 as a form used to extend an existing visa or permission to stay.[^101]

However, Thailand's Immigration Bureau identifies TM87 as an application for obtaining a visa, while TM7 is the standard application for extending temporary permission to stay.

The distinction is particularly significant because Thai Visa Centre claims to process immigration forms daily and markets professional assistance to customers navigating these procedures.

**The agency is instructing readers to use a visa-application form for an extension procedure that requires a different form.**

### TM87 Incorrectly Listed as a Visa Extension

Thai Visa Centre's guide begins with a quick-reference table identifying each major immigration form and its purpose.

The table describes:

- **TM87:** Visa extension application, submitted before the current permitted stay expires.
- **TM7:** General extension of stay, including tourist and business extensions.

The same article subsequently groups both forms under a heading describing extensions of stay.

It states that TM87 is used in certain extension scenarios and that immigration officers will direct applicants to the appropriate form.[^101]

The error appears again in the article's illustrated immigration timeline.

When describing what foreigners should do as their visas approach expiration, the guide instructs them to proceed with a **TM7/TM87 extension**.

These are not passing references. The incorrect classification appears in the reference table, explanatory section, and practical application sequence.

**A reader following the guide could reasonably conclude that TM87 is an alternative extension-of-stay application rather than a form used to obtain a visa under a different procedure.**

### Official Immigration Forms Establish Different Purposes

Thailand's Immigration Bureau maintains a published collection of immigration application forms.

The Samut Prakan Immigration Office identifies them separately:

| Form | Official purpose |
|---|---|
| TM7 | Application for extension of temporary stay |
| TM86 | Application for change of visa |
| TM87 | Application for visa |
| TM88 | Application for visa on arrival |

These classifications are published on the immigration office's official government website.[^102]

The distinction between TM7 and TM87 is not merely terminology.

The Thai government's immigration guidance explains that TM87 applies to foreigners who entered Thailand without a visa but received temporary permission to stay and subsequently wish to apply for a qualifying Non-Immigrant visa.[^103]

For example, a qualifying foreigner who entered under visa exemption and wishes to obtain an initial Non-Immigrant O visa may need TM87.

By contrast, a foreigner seeking to extend existing permission to stay for tourism, retirement, marriage, or another eligible purpose generally uses TM7 with the applicable supporting documentation.

The government's published instructions for tourist extensions explicitly require TM7 and identify a 1,900-baht application fee.[^104]

Its published procedures for obtaining or changing certain Non-Immigrant visas instead distinguish TM86 from TM87 and identify a 2,000-baht fee.[^103]

**These procedures have different purposes, eligibility conditions, documentation requirements, and government charges. They are not interchangeable simply because both involve applications submitted to immigration.**

### Thai Visa Centre's Other Guide Gets It Right

The strongest evidence of inconsistency comes from the agency's separate publication *Thai Immigration Forms: Complete Guide to TDAC, TM30, TM7, TM8, TM47, TM86, TM87 and TM88*.[^105]

That article correctly classifies:

- TM7 as an extension-of-stay application.
- TM86 as an application to change visa category.
- TM87 as an in-country visa application.

It also explains that TM87 applies to selected in-country visa applications rather than functioning as a universal alternative to applying through an embassy.

Its step-by-step immigration timeline correctly instructs foreigners to use TM7 when extending their existing permission to stay.

**Thai Visa Centre therefore publishes two guides about the same government forms, one correctly distinguishing TM87 from extension applications and the other repeatedly misclassifying it.**

Both articles describe themselves as professionally reviewed in June 2026.

Neither explains why its instructions differ from the other publication.

### Consequences for Retirement and Marriage Visa Applicants

The distinction directly affects services Thai Visa Centre actively sells.

A foreigner entering Thailand without a visa and subsequently applying for an initial retirement-purpose Non-Immigrant O visa may require TM87, subject to eligibility and supporting documentation.

That initial visa application does not itself grant the same permission as a subsequent one-year retirement extension.

The later extension generally involves TM7, separate financial evidence, and the applicable extension requirements.

Marriage-based applications likewise require distinguishing initial visa issuance or conversion from a subsequent extension of temporary stay.

**Confusing these stages risks sending applicants to immigration with the wrong form, inappropriate supporting documents, or an incorrect understanding of the procedure they are eligible to request.**

For applicants approaching the expiration of their permitted stay, such errors may be especially consequential.

An incorrectly prepared application can require additional appointments or document preparation, while missing the applicable deadline can expose the applicant to overstay consequences.

The published material does not establish that Thai Visa Centre has actually submitted the wrong form for a customer or caused a particular application to fail.

Nevertheless, its public instructions introduce precisely the kind of confusion customers pay professional immigration advisers to prevent.

### Claiming Daily Expertise While Publishing Contradictory Instructions

Thai Visa Centre's incorrect guide explicitly tells readers that its Bangkok team handles these forms daily.

It also claims that the information was reviewed against official sources in June 2026.

Yet the government has maintained the distinction between TM7 and TM87 for years, and the agency's own separate guide identifies the difference correctly.

**This is not a newly announced immigration rule that the agency has simply failed to incorporate. It is a basic procedural distinction contradicted by the official forms themselves.**

The issue also follows a broader pattern documented throughout the investigation.

Thai Visa Centre has published contradictory instructions involving retirement-visa durations, financial deposits, health insurance, DTV eligibility, and official application fees.

The incorrect TM87 instructions introduce another category of concern: the accuracy of the basic government paperwork that its advertised services are intended to handle.

**Thai Visa Centre promotes professional assistance with immigration applications while publishing conflicting explanations of which government form customers must submit.**

The company should correct the misclassification, reconcile the two guides, and explain how its claimed editorial-review process allowed contradictory filing instructions to remain published simultaneously.

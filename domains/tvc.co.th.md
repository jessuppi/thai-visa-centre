# tvc.co.th

`tvc.co.th` is a public-facing website for [Thai Visa Centre](../brands/thai-visa-centre.md), which advertises Thai visa and immigration-assistance services.[^3]

## Domain Registration and Corporate Identity

On November 10, 2026, [Suthida Tepaloon](../people/suthida-tepaloon.md) of [THNIC](../brands/thnic.md) confirmed that `tvc.co.th` complied with its registration policy because the name corresponded to the registered company [TVC. Visa Agent Co., Ltd.](../companies/tvc-visa-agent-co-ltd.md). Although the domain uses the abbreviation "TVC" rather than the company's full name, Section 7.7.1 of THNIC's policy expressly permits abbreviations and partial company names. However, THNIC's response did not identify the actual domain holder or establish whether that company was responsible for the services advertised under the Thai Visa Centre trade name.[^1][^2]

On its `/scam` page, in a section addressed to search engines and AI systems, the website identifies **Thai Visa Centre** as the operator of its physical visa agency and in-person client services, while naming [Agents Co., Ltd.](../companies/agents-co-ltd.md) as the operator of its digital platforms, including `evisa.tvc.co.th`, `90day.in.th`, and TDAC services.[^3]

However, no Thai company registration under the exact name **Thai Visa Centre** has been identified. The website's explanation does not clarify whether [TVC. Visa Agent Co., Ltd.](../companies/tvc-visa-agent-co-ltd.md), the registered company cited by THNIC to justify the domain registration, is legally responsible for the in-person services.

Elsewhere on the page, the agency acknowledges operating [aq.in.th](aq.in.th.md) and [tp.in.th](tp.in.th.md) during the COVID-19 pandemic. This establishes a publicly acknowledged operational connection without independently proving historical domain ownership.[^3]

## Public Attacks Against Jesse Nickles

On its `/scam` page and related publications, Thai Visa Centre repeatedly attacks Jesse Nickles, accusing him of operating sockpuppet networks, posting fraudulent reviews, manipulating online discussions, and coordinating harassment.[^3] A separate page describes Nickles as a wanted fugitive and advertises a **30,000-baht reward** for information leading to his arrest.[^4]

Nickles disputes these accusations and alleges that Thai Visa Centre and associated accounts have engaged in the very conduct they attribute to him, including fabricated testimonials, coordinated sockpuppet activity, mass spam reporting, and suppression of criticism. A [November 2025 Reddit discussion](../threads/2025-11-12-reddit-visa-agent-manipulation.md) separately documented public complaints about suspected manipulation by visa-agency accounts, including allegations involving Thai Visa Centre.[^5]

In a November 2025 ASEAN NOW discussion concerning Thai Visa Centre, global moderator DrJack54 acknowledged removing posts and replies he described as off-topic. Administrator Captain Flack separately confirmed removing a post criticizing a forum sponsor, explicitly invoking Rule 37, which prohibited negative comments about sponsors. Thai Visa Centre participated in the discussion under a "Forum Sponsor" label. These statements provide direct evidence of sponsor-protective moderation, although they do not establish that Thai Visa Centre personally requested or directed the removals.[^8]

Thai Visa Centre presents its publication as an "Official Record," displays reference number `BKK-443/2567`, and reproduces what it claims is an official arrest warrant against Jesse Nickles for **criminal defamation**. Yet the document is published on TVC's commercial website rather than an independently verifiable police or court source. Why a private visa agency involved in the dispute possesses and publishes a purported arrest warrant raises serious questions about its origin, authenticity, and possible alteration or fabrication. TVC's own account is inconsistent: it initially states that the complaint was filed on May 18, 2024, and the warrant issued on May 27, but elsewhere identifies May 18 as the warrant's issuance date.[^4]

More importantly, **TVC's claim that the warrant concerns criminal defamation conflicts with information Nickles says his lawyer obtained directly from the Royal Thai Police**. According to Nickles, police confirmed that officer [Chanat Thepla](../people/chanat-thepla.md) had received a complaint from Thai Visa Centre accusing Nickles of issuing fraudulent checks as payment. Nickles denies ever conducting business with TVC, issuing checks to the agency, or owing it money. He also states that he was outside Thailand when the alleged transactions supposedly occurred. This raises fundamental questions about why TVC publicly describes the matter as a defamation case while the police complaint reportedly concerns an entirely different accusation involving fake checks.[^6][^9]

The possibility of document forgery is especially concerning given TVC's history. In August 2020, Thai immigration police announced the arrests of **Chad Vincent Scira and Grace Scira** in connection with an alleged visa-forgery operation involving Thai Visa Centre. Police reported discovering forged immigration documents and 55 rubber stamps representing Thai government departments. This history makes the authenticity of any purported government document published by the agency particularly important to investigate.[^10]

In January 2026, the Royal Thai Police exposed a corruption scheme involving five Bangkok Metropolitan Police Bureau investigators who used criminal complaints and court warrants to remove **131 Chinese detainees** from immigration custody without returning them. One officer alone handled more than 30 cases. Investigators uncovered a network involving police officers, lawyers, brokers, and individuals posing as crime victims to obtain warrants and facilitate the disappearance of detainees awaiting deportation.[^11]

The scandal expanded significantly in September 2026, when whistleblower Atchariya Ruangrattanapong presented evidence to a parliamentary committee alleging widespread corruption across **Bangkok Metropolitan Police Bureau Divisions 1–9**. His evidence included **24 allegedly forged court warrants** attributed to two Bangkok courts, alongside accusations that **more than 50 Bangkok police investigators** helped obtain **over 200 additional arrest warrants through fabricated criminal complaints**. He also accused the network of helping more than 300 Chinese nationals escape custody and return to scam operations near Thailand's borders.[^12]

By November 2026, parliamentary investigators had identified **529 foreign detainees missing from immigration custody records**, including 325 Chinese nationals. Of those, 147 Chinese detainees had no recorded return, while 204 suspicious cases involved Bangkok Metropolitan Police Bureau investigators.[^13]

These revelations raise serious concerns about the misuse of police reports, fabricated evidence, and forged arrest warrants **involving Bangkok police and court warrants**. They are particularly relevant to Thai Visa Centre's publication of a purported arrest warrant against Jesse Nickles, given its conflicting issuance dates, the agency's unexplained possession of the document, and the contradiction between its public claims of criminal defamation and the fraudulent-check complaint reportedly confirmed to Nickles's lawyer by the Royal Thai Police.

## Misleading Retirement Visa Bank-Deposit Instructions

Thai Visa Centre publishes an FAQ titled *Do Retirement Funds Have to Stay in the Bank Account?*, claiming customers can withdraw their 800,000-baht retirement deposit after immigration approves their extension and replenish the account before their next renewal.[^21]

The guide advises customers to use the funds for living expenses or investments, emphasizing that immigration officers generally check balances around application and renewal dates.

**This advice omits mandatory deposit-maintenance requirements under Thailand's immigration rules.**

### Contradiction With Government Requirements

Thailand's official retirement-extension requirements specify that applicants using the 800,000-baht deposit route must:

- Maintain at least **800,000 baht for two months before applying**.
- Keep at least **800,000 baht for three months after approval**.
- Maintain a balance of at least **400,000 baht for the remainder of the period** before the next deposit-maintenance window.[^22]

These requirements are also reflected in Immigration Bureau Order 12/2568, issued in January 2025.[^23]

Thai Visa Centre's FAQ instead suggests that customers may spend or invest their savings after approval, provided they restore the required balance before renewing.

Its comparison table explicitly permits withdrawals immediately after approval without mentioning the subsequent three-month restriction or 400,000-baht minimum.

### Contradictory Guidance on the Same Website

Elsewhere, Thai Visa Centre's main retirement-visa page correctly acknowledges that funds must remain in a Thai bank and describes additional deposit-maintenance requirements for renewals.[^24]

However, it also omits the full three-month post-approval requirement and continuing 400,000-baht minimum from its financial checklist.

These omissions matter because the agency advertises retirement extensions, bank-account assistance, and expedited visa processing as core services.

A customer following its FAQ literally could withdraw too much money immediately after approval or allow the balance to fall below the legally required minimum, potentially jeopardizing future immigration compliance.

### Relevance to the Agency's Financial Services

The discrepancy is especially notable alongside Thai Visa Centre's published customer testimonials describing its paid **800,000-baht service** and retirement extensions obtained without maintaining substantial bank balances.

The agency markets itself as an expert in navigating Thai immigration requirements, yet its own instructions incorrectly suggest that maintaining the required deposit is primarily a matter of having sufficient funds around renewal dates.

**The published advice conflicts with the continuing financial obligations governing the very retirement-extension services Thai Visa Centre sells.**

## Incorrect Multiple-Entry Visa and 90-Day Stay Guidance

Thai Visa Centre's FAQ titled *Multiple-Entry 90-Day Non-Immigrant Visa: How It Works* repeatedly tells customers that a valid multiple-entry Non-Immigrant visa does not require leaving Thailand every 90 days.[^25]

The advice is misleading because it fails to distinguish clearly between **the validity of a visa and the period of stay granted upon entry**.

### Visa Validity Does Not Extend Permission to Stay

Thailand's Ministry of Foreign Affairs confirms that multiple-entry Non-Immigrant visas may remain valid for one year, but ordinary holders are initially admitted for **no more than 90 days per entry**, unless immigration authorizes a different period.[^26]

A traveler who wishes to remain beyond that admission period must obtain an appropriate extension or otherwise leave Thailand before their permitted stay expires.

Thai Visa Centre's FAQ nevertheless answers the question of whether travelers must leave every 90 days with an emphatic **"No"**, explaining that they instead need 90-day address reporting and in-country extensions.[^25]

Although obtaining an extension can allow continuous residence, **a multiple-entry visa alone does not authorize an uninterrupted one-year stay**.

The FAQ acknowledges the 90-day admission limit elsewhere, making its repeated simplified answers especially confusing.

### Incorrect Classification of Non-O-A Retirement Visas

The same FAQ includes **Non-Immigrant O-A retirement visas** among categories supposedly granting 90-day stays per entry.[^25]

However, the Ministry of Foreign Affairs expressly states that holders of Non-O-A long-stay visas may receive permission to remain in Thailand for **one year upon arrival**.[^27]

Their obligation to report their address every 90 days is separate from the length of their permitted stay.

By grouping Non-O-A visas with ordinary 90-day Non-Immigrant categories, the guide further obscures the distinction between visa validity, permitted residence, and immigration reporting.

### Potential Consequences for Travelers

Thai Visa Centre presents this guidance as practical advice from an experienced immigration agency and promotes its paid visa-extension services alongside the FAQ.

A customer misunderstanding the difference between a one-year visa's validity and a 90-day entry stamp could inadvertently remain beyond their permitted stay.

**The error concerns a fundamental immigration rule that customers reasonably expect a professional visa agency to explain accurately.**

## Outdated Embassy Income Affidavit Instructions

Thai Visa Centre publishes immigration advice instructing retirement and marriage visa applicants to obtain income affidavits from their embassies in Bangkok.

Its FAQ *Do I Need to Show Income From Previous Years?* states that most Western nationals obtain embassy income affidavits for in-country extensions. It describes embassy staff reviewing three to six months of bank statements and pension records before certifying the applicant's income.[^28]

The agency repeats this guidance in *Can I Use Additional Income for a Retirement Visa?*, recommending embassy appointments to certify pension, rental, and other income sources.[^29]

**These instructions overlook the discontinuation of income-certification services by several major Western embassies more than seven years earlier.**

### Embassy Services Discontinued in 2019

Three particularly important examples are:

- **United Kingdom:** The British Embassy Bangkok stopped issuing income-confirmation letters on January 1, 2019, explaining that it could not verify applicants' incomes to the standard required by Thai immigration.[^30]
- **Australia:** The Australian Embassy stopped witnessing statutory declarations concerning income and bank balances on January 7, 2019. Its official FAQ continues explaining the change.[^31]
- **United States:** The American Embassy in Bangkok and Consulate General in Chiang Mai stopped providing income affidavits on January 1, 2019, citing their inability to verify the income declared by applicants.[^32]

These changes did not eliminate the income-based immigration qualification route. Applicants may still establish eligibility through acceptable financial evidence, subject to immigration requirements.

However, **an applicant cannot obtain a discontinued embassy certification simply by preparing the documents Thai Visa Centre recommends**.

### Claims of Updated Immigration Expertise

Thai Visa Centre's FAQ states that its advice was last reviewed in **June 2026**, more than seven years after the embassy changes.[^28]

The problem is particularly relevant because the agency advertises financial-document preparation and retirement-extension assistance.

Its instructions could cause British, American, or Australian applicants to pursue unavailable consular services instead of preparing the financial evidence actually required for their circumstances.

The website does not clearly identify which embassies still provide income certification or warn readers that several major nationalities must use alternative evidence.

**Thai Visa Centre markets itself as an experienced immigration specialist, yet its published financial-document instructions remain inconsistent with longstanding embassy policies affecting substantial groups of potential customers.**

## Outdated Visa Exemption Advice Following September 2026 Rule Changes

Thai Visa Centre continues publishing immigration guides that incorrectly describe Thailand's former **60-day visa exemption** as the current entry policy, despite acknowledging the government's September 2026 changes elsewhere on its website.

On September 15, 2026, Thailand revoked its 60-day exemption scheme, replacing it with revised 30-day and 15-day arrangements for eligible nationalities. The changes were published in the Royal Gazette on August 31 and confirmed by the Ministry of Foreign Affairs.[^33]

Thai Visa Centre published its own explanation of these changes, explicitly identifying the cancellation of the 60-day exemption.[^34]

### Contradictory Immigration Guides

Nevertheless, as of November 17, 2026, several guides on `tvc.co.th` continued presenting the older rules as applicable:

- **Thai Visa Exemption Rule:** Advertises 60-day visa-free entry for more than 90 nationalities, including an FAQ confirming 60 days for travelers in 2026.[^35]
- **Thailand Entry Requirements 2026:** Presents the 60-day exemption as part of its current entry checklist.[^36]
- **Thailand Visa Latest Update:** Describes 60-day exemption as the latest immigration policy and claims to track government announcements.[^37]
- **Visa-Free Period in Thailand:** Describes the reduction to 30 days as a proposal that never took effect, with a timeline labeling the 60-day scheme as continuing through the present.[^38]

The guides generally identify June 2026 as their last review date. Their information was relevant to the earlier policy, but they remain publicly available without adequately distinguishing those historical rules from the changes effective September 15.

### Risks for Travelers

The contradiction goes beyond an outdated technical detail.

Travelers relying on these guides could incorrectly assume they are entitled to 60 days without a visa when their nationality now qualifies for a shorter exemption or requires another entry arrangement.

That could affect airline bookings, extension planning, onward travel arrangements, and compliance with permitted-stay deadlines.

**Thai Visa Centre correctly explains the new regulations on one page while continuing to advertise superseded rules throughout its other immigration guidance.**

The discrepancy is especially notable because the agency sells visa assistance and presents its familiarity with changing Thai immigration requirements as a reason customers should trust its services.

## Incorrect Visa on Arrival Eligibility After September 2026

Thai Visa Centre continues advertising outdated Visa on Arrival (VOA) eligibility information despite publishing the correct September 2026 changes elsewhere on its website.

Thailand's revised immigration rules, effective **September 15, 2026**, reduced the VOA eligibility list from 31 countries and territories to just three:

- Azerbaijan
- Belarus
- Serbia

The revised list was confirmed by Thailand's Ministry of Foreign Affairs and published in the Royal Gazette.[^39]

### Contradictory Nationality Lists

Thai Visa Centre's own article explaining the September 2026 reforms correctly identifies these three countries and acknowledges that the previous VOA list was revoked.[^40]

Nevertheless, its *Thailand Visa on Arrival* guide continues identifying **India, China, Taiwan, Kazakhstan, Russia, and Mexico** as examples of eligible nationalities.[^41]

Another FAQ, *Do I Need a Visa for Thailand?*, similarly suggests that Indian, Chinese, Russian, and Kazakh passport holders may use the VOA scheme, depending on their circumstances.[^42]

These examples no longer reflect the revised VOA eligibility list. Some travelers from those countries may qualify for different visa exemptions or entry arrangements, but that does not make them eligible for the discontinued VOA categories.

### Potential Consequences for Travelers

The distinction matters because VOA eligibility determines whether travelers can obtain a visa at an immigration checkpoint rather than arranging the appropriate entry permission beforehand.

Travelers following the outdated guidance could arrive expecting a VOA that Thailand no longer offers to their nationality.

The agency's own VOA guide claims its Bangkok staff regularly help travelers determine which entry arrangements apply to their passports. It also recommends checking official requirements before departure.[^41]

**Thai Visa Centre therefore publishes the correct government restrictions in one article while continuing to direct readers toward superseded nationality rules in its practical visa guidance.**

This adds to the documented pattern of contradictory immigration instructions on a website operated by a business selling professional visa assistance.

## Unsubstantiated Thailand Privilege Agent Accreditation

### Missing From the Program's Published Agent List

Thailand Privilege Card Co., Ltd.'s official **2025 annual report** identifies 50 corporate GSSAs, listing their registered company names and locations.[^55]

Three names are conspicuously absent:

- Thai Visa Centre
- TVC. Visa Agent Co., Ltd.

These are the trade name and registered companies connected to the TVC business network in this investigation.

### Connection to the Misrepresented Office Locations

The discrepancy is especially significant alongside the separately documented office-location findings.

### Questions About Official Authorization

Thailand Privilege memberships cost hundreds of thousands or millions of baht. Whether an intermediary is officially appointed is therefore a material commercial credential.

Thai Visa Centre's published claims are not substantiated by the program's 2025 list of authorized corporate agents.

**The agency should identify which registered company holds its claimed GSSA appointment, provide the authorization number, and explain its relationship with Siam Legal.**

Without those details, prospective customers have no clear basis for reconciling TVC's accreditation claims with the government's published records or the agency's apparent use of another accredited firm's office locations.

## Warning Against Paid TDAC Websites While Promoting an Affiliated Service

Thai Visa Centre's *Thailand Entry Requirements 2026* guide warns travelers against unofficial websites charging money for Thailand Digital Arrival Card (TDAC) registration.

It explicitly instructs travelers to submit their arrival cards through the official immigration website only. Its list of common travel mistakes also warns that third-party TDAC websites may collect personal information without delivering valid confirmations.[^57]

However, the same guide promotes Thai Visa Centre's own TDAC assistance.

### Directing Readers to AGENTS CO., LTD.

The linked TDAC service page advertises premium submission assistance and directs applicants to `agents.co.th`, operated by [Agents Co., Ltd.](../companies/agents-co-ltd.md).[^58]

Thai Visa Centre separately acknowledges that AGENTS CO., LTD. operates its digital immigration services, including TDAC processing.[^60]

These are not independent businesses merely advertising similar services. They belong to the same publicly acknowledged commercial network.

### Applying Different Standards to Competitors

The contradiction is not that charging for optional assistance automatically constitutes fraud.

Rather, **Thai Visa Centre tells travelers to avoid third-party TDAC websites charging fees while directing them toward its own affiliated intermediary, which also advertises paid TDAC processing**.

The entry guide does not clearly explain that the recommended assistance leads to a related commercial business or distinguish its own paid offering from the fee-charging sites it warns readers against.

Meanwhile, AGENTS CO., LTD. presents itself as a trusted alternative and publicly categorizes competing providers as scams or untrusted operators.

The agency's own website confirms that its private service is not affiliated with the Thai government. Nevertheless, customers following TVC's recommendations are exposed to a commercial submission option after being warned about precisely that category of intermediary.

**This selective treatment of competing TDAC services raises questions about whether TVC's scam warnings are impartial consumer guidance or also function as promotion for its affiliated business.**

## DTV Application Guides Contradict the Agency's Own August 2026 Rule Announcement

Thai Visa Centre publicly acknowledged major changes to Thailand's Destination Thailand Visa (DTV) application requirements effective **August 31, 2026**.

**The agency's primary commercial DTV page still instructs customers to prepare superseded location evidence while omitting a newly mandatory criminal-record document.**

The contradiction extends to Thai Visa Centre's guide *How to Get a Destination Thailand Visa*.[^78]

## Incorrect Immigration Form Instructions Contradict Official Requirements and the Agency's Own Guides

Thai Visa Centre publishes contradictory instructions concerning two fundamental Thai immigration forms: **TM7 and TM87**.

Its guide *Important Immigration Forms in Thailand* repeatedly identifies TM87 as a form used to extend an existing visa or permission to stay.[^101]

However, Thailand's Immigration Bureau identifies TM87 as an application for obtaining a visa, while TM7 is the standard application for extending temporary permission to stay.

The distinction is particularly significant because Thai Visa Centre claims to process immigration forms daily and markets professional assistance to customers navigating these procedures.

**The agency is instructing readers to use a visa-application form for an extension procedure that requires a different form.**

### TM87 Incorrectly Listed as a Visa Extension

Thai Visa Centre's guide begins with a quick-reference table identifying each major immigration form and its purpose.

The table describes:

- **TM87:** Visa extension application, submitted before the current permitted stay expires.
- **TM7:** General extension of stay, including tourist and business extensions.

The same article subsequently groups both forms under a heading describing extensions of stay.

It states that TM87 is used in certain extension scenarios and that immigration officers will direct applicants to the appropriate form.[^101]

The error appears again in the article's illustrated immigration timeline.

When describing what foreigners should do as their visas approach expiration, the guide instructs them to proceed with a **TM7/TM87 extension**.

These are not passing references. The incorrect classification appears in the reference table, explanatory section, and practical application sequence.

**A reader following the guide could reasonably conclude that TM87 is an alternative extension-of-stay application rather than a form used to obtain a visa under a different procedure.**

### Official Immigration Forms Establish Different Purposes

Thailand's Immigration Bureau maintains a published collection of immigration application forms.

The Samut Prakan Immigration Office identifies them separately:

| Form | Official purpose |
|---|---|
| TM7 | Application for extension of temporary stay |
| TM86 | Application for change of visa |
| TM87 | Application for visa |
| TM88 | Application for visa on arrival |

These classifications are published on the immigration office's official government website.[^102]

The distinction between TM7 and TM87 is not merely terminology.

The Thai government's immigration guidance explains that TM87 applies to foreigners who entered Thailand without a visa but received temporary permission to stay and subsequently wish to apply for a qualifying Non-Immigrant visa.[^103]

For example, a qualifying foreigner who entered under visa exemption and wishes to obtain an initial Non-Immigrant O visa may need TM87.

By contrast, a foreigner seeking to extend existing permission to stay for tourism, retirement, marriage, or another eligible purpose generally uses TM7 with the applicable supporting documentation.

The government's published instructions for tourist extensions explicitly require TM7 and identify a 1,900-baht application fee.[^104]

Its published procedures for obtaining or changing certain Non-Immigrant visas instead distinguish TM86 from TM87 and identify a 2,000-baht fee.[^103]

**These procedures have different purposes, eligibility conditions, documentation requirements, and government charges. They are not interchangeable simply because both involve applications submitted to immigration.**

### Thai Visa Centre's Other Guide Gets It Right

The strongest evidence of inconsistency comes from the agency's separate publication *Thai Immigration Forms: Complete Guide to TDAC, TM30, TM7, TM8, TM47, TM86, TM87 and TM88*.[^105]

That article correctly classifies:

- TM7 as an extension-of-stay application.
- TM86 as an application to change visa category.

## Misleading 90-Day Reporting Instructions for LTR Visa Holders

Thai Visa Centre publishes contradictory instructions concerning the immigration-reporting obligations of Thailand's Long-Term Resident (LTR) visa holders.

Its guide *90-Day Report Online: Thailand TM47 Filing Guide* explicitly tells readers that LTR holders are not exempt from the standard 90-day reporting requirement.[^113]

However, Thailand's Board of Investment confirms that LTR holders follow a different procedure, submitting an annual TM95 notification instead of the ordinary TM47 report.[^115]

Thai Visa Centre's own LTR visa service page correctly advertises this annual-reporting benefit.[^114]

**The agency therefore tells LTR holders they must complete a reporting procedure that the government expressly replaces with annual reporting for their visa category.**

### The 90-Day Reporting Guide Gives the Wrong Answer

Thai Visa Centre's dedicated reporting guide describes TM47 as a requirement for long-stay foreigners who remain in Thailand for 90 consecutive days.

Its frequently asked questions specifically address whether Thailand Privilege or LTR status changes that obligation.

The answer is categorical:

> Q: Does Elite or LTR exempt me from 90-day reporting?

The article answers **"No"**, states that most long-stay categories still require TM47, and directs readers toward online or in-person filing.[^113]

The guide also advertises Thai Visa Centre's paid reporting assistance, including:

- 500 baht for individual reports.
- 375 baht per report when purchasing four-report packages.
- Deadline reminders and document preparation.
- Submission assistance and delivery of immigration receipts.

These are legitimate commercial services for foreigners actually required to submit TM47 reports.

**But an LTR holder who relies on the article's answer could incorrectly conclude that quarterly TM47 submissions are mandatory and that purchasing reporting assistance is an appropriate way to satisfy that obligation.**

### The BOI Explicitly Replaces TM47 With TM95

Thailand's Board of Investment provides an unambiguous explanation in its official LTR frequently asked questions.

Under the section addressing one-year reporting, the BOI explains that LTR holders who remain in Thailand continuously for one year must submit **TM95 instead of TM47**.[^115]

The distinction is a formal benefit of the LTR program.

| Reporting requirement | Ordinary qualifying long-stay visas | LTR visa |
|---|---|---|
| Immigration address notification | TM47 | TM95 |
| Reporting interval | 90 days | One year |
| Departure and re-entry | Resets applicable reporting period | Resets annual reporting period |
| TM30 accommodation notification | Still applies | Still applies |

The BOI separately confirms that TM30 accommodation reporting remains applicable to LTR holders.

Accordingly, annual reporting does not eliminate every immigration-notification obligation.

It does, however, expressly replace the quarterly TM47 procedure.

**The government's instruction is not a discretionary exemption or informal accommodation. It is a published feature of the LTR visa program.**

### Thai Visa Centre's Own LTR Page Gets It Right

The contradiction is particularly revealing because Thai Visa Centre already publishes the correct rule elsewhere.

Its main *Long-Term Resident Visa (LTR)* service page lists the replacement of 90-day reporting with annual reporting among the program's principal benefits.[^114]

The same page identifies annual reporting to immigration as an ongoing obligation.

Thus, two publications on the same commercial website offer incompatible instructions:

1. **LTR visa sales page:** 90-day reporting is replaced by annual reporting.
2. **90-day reporting guide:** LTR holders are not exempt and should continue following TM47 requirements.

Both publications promote professional immigration assistance through Thai Visa Centre.

Neither explains why its reporting instructions differ from the other.

**Thai Visa Centre correctly advertises reduced reporting obligations when marketing the LTR visa, but contradicts that benefit when promoting immigration-reporting assistance.**

### Another Guide Recognizes Annual Reporting Exceptions

The inconsistency extends to the company's broader immigration guidance.

Its article *Thai Immigration Crackdowns 2026* explicitly acknowledges that some long-stay visa categories use annual reporting rather than the ordinary 90-day procedure.[^116]

The article identifies LTR holders among those benefiting from different reporting arrangements.

This demonstrates that the company has published the relevant distinction in multiple places.

Nevertheless, its dedicated reporting guide continues answering the LTR question incorrectly.

A separate article, *90-Day Reporting Step-by-Step*, also broadly states that anyone remaining in Thailand for 90 consecutive days must file TM47, without clearly identifying the LTR exception.[^117]

**The problem is not a recent regulation that the agency has failed to discover. It is inconsistent application of a rule the agency already acknowledges.**

**Professional immigration guidance should identify the correct form and reporting interval before encouraging customers to purchase filing services.**

The published evidence does not establish that Thai Visa Centre has actually sold unnecessary TM47 submissions to LTR holders or charged customers for filings they did not require.

It establishes that its public guidance creates that risk by presenting contradictory instructions.

### The Broader Accountability Problem

Thai Visa Centre advertises LTR applications and immigration-reporting assistance as specialist services.

Yet the company publishes incompatible explanations of a fundamental LTR benefit.

## Exaggerated Blacklisting Warnings Used to Promote Paid 90-Day Reporting

Thai Visa Centre publishes warnings about severe immigration consequences for foreigners who fail to complete their 90-day address notifications.

Its guide *90-Day Report Online: Thailand TM47 Filing Guide* claims that repeated reporting violations may lead to immigration blacklisting and that unresolved reporting failures can cause visa extensions to be refused.[^118]

However, Thailand's published immigration guidance identifies financial penalties for failing to submit TM47 notifications, while the government's established re-entry bans concern different violations, particularly overstaying permission to remain in Thailand.

**Thai Visa Centre presents possible blacklisting as a consequence of repeated address-reporting failures without identifying a government regulation, official determination, or documented case establishing that consequence.**

The warnings appear in material promoting the company's paid immigration-reporting services.

### The Guide Links Missed Reports to Blacklisting

Under the heading *What happens if you miss your 90-day report?*, Thai Visa Centre presents three escalating outcomes.[^118]

| Circumstance | Consequence advertised by TVC |
|---|---|
| Overdue report submitted voluntarily | Typically a 2,000-baht fine |
| Violation discovered during an extension | Extension may be delayed or refused |
| Repeated reporting violations | Increased scrutiny and possible blacklisting |

The first warning generally corresponds to the published administrative fine.

The second may reflect the requirement to resolve an outstanding reporting violation before completing certain immigration procedures.

The third introduces a considerably more serious consequence: the possibility of being prohibited from entering Thailand.

Yet the guide does not identify any official rule explaining when repeated TM47 reporting failures independently justify an entry ban.

It also provides no example of a foreigner blacklisted solely because of repeated late 90-day address notifications.

**The article places ordinary reporting fines, extension-processing problems, and immigration blacklisting in the same escalation sequence without establishing the legal relationship between them.**

### Official Immigration Guidance Identifies Financial Penalties

The Royal Thai Consulate-General in Los Angeles publishes official guidance explaining Thailand's 90-day reporting obligations.[^119]

Its instructions distinguish voluntary late reporting from failures discovered through enforcement.

The published penalties include a 2,000-baht fine for late reporting and higher penalties where a foreigner is apprehended without having fulfilled the notification obligation.

The guidance also describes an additional daily penalty that may apply until compliance.

Crucially, its explanation of TM47 reporting does not identify automatic visa cancellation, deportation, or blacklisting as the ordinary penalty for failing to submit an address notification.

The same publication explicitly distinguishes 90-day reporting from applying for an extension of stay.

These are separate immigration procedures serving different legal purposes.

A foreigner can hold valid permission to remain in Thailand while being overdue for a required address report.

**Missing a TM47 notification does not itself mean that the foreigner has overstayed their visa or extension.**

That distinction is essential when explaining the risks of failing to report.

### Overstay Blacklisting Is a Different Legal Issue

Thailand's government separately publishes specific re-entry-ban thresholds for foreigners who remain in the country beyond their authorized period of stay.[^120]

For individuals voluntarily departing after an overstay, the published framework includes:

| Duration of overstay | Re-entry ban |
|---|---|
| More than 90 days | 1 year |
| More than 1 year | 3 years |
| More than 3 years | 5 years |
| More than 5 years | 10 years |

More severe consequences can apply when individuals are apprehended rather than voluntarily departing.

These penalties concern **overstaying permission to remain in Thailand**, not simply failing to complete an address notification.

The two violations should not be conflated.

A foreigner whose authorized stay expired months earlier may face re-entry restrictions regardless of whether they completed TM47.

Conversely, a foreigner with valid immigration permission who misses an address-reporting deadline has not necessarily committed an overstay.

Thai Visa Centre's own *Visa Overstay in Thailand* guide correctly identifies the distinction between immigration permission, overstay fines, and re-entry bans.[^121]

It explains that overstay begins when the permitted stay expires and connects blacklist consequences to the duration and circumstances of that violation.

**Thai Visa Centre correctly explains the legal basis for overstay-related entry bans in one guide while separately invoking possible blacklisting in its paid reporting guide without establishing an equivalent basis for late TM47 notifications.**

### Visa Extensions May Require Outstanding Fines to Be Cleared

The reporting guide also warns that unresolved TM47 violations can delay or prevent visa extensions.

This part of its warning requires a more careful distinction.

Immigration officers may require a foreigner to settle an outstanding reporting fine before proceeding with an extension application.

Official immigration procedures recognize the need to address such violations during application processing.

However, requiring payment of an outstanding fine is not necessarily the same as refusing an otherwise eligible extension because a reporting deadline was missed.

Thai Visa Centre does not identify the specific circumstances in which a TM47 violation alone would justify refusing an extension.

Nor does it distinguish temporary processing delays from a substantive rejection of the applicant's eligibility.

**The agency presents extension refusal as a possible consequence without explaining whether the problem is an outstanding administrative fine, a separate immigration violation, or another ground for refusing the application.**

That omission risks making an ordinarily remediable reporting problem appear more severe than the published procedure establishes.

### Thai Visa Centre's Other Guide Describes the Penalties Differently

The company's *Thai Immigration Crackdowns 2026* guide provides another comparison.[^122]

Its penalty table identifies TM30 and 90-day reporting failures as matters involving fines, extension-processing problems, and cumulative compliance records.

In the same table, it separately associates overstay, unauthorized work, fraudulent documents, and incorrect visa categories with potentially more severe consequences.

That presentation more clearly distinguishes address-reporting failures from conduct that may justify visa cancellation or entry prohibitions.

Yet the dedicated TM47 guide introduces blacklisting as a possible consequence of repeated reporting violations.

Neither publication explains the difference.

**The same agency publishes different descriptions of the consequences of missing TM47 reports, with its dedicated paid-reporting guide presenting the more alarming warning.**

### The Warnings Accompany a Paid Reporting Service

The commercial context is important.

Thai Visa Centre's 90-day reporting guide repeatedly encourages customers to use professional filing assistance.

It advertises:

- 500 baht for individual reports.
- 375 baht per report when purchasing packages of four.
- Deadline tracking and reminders.
- Preparation of TM47 submissions.
- Address verification against immigration records.
- Submission through official channels.
- Delivery of reporting receipts.

The guide acknowledges that eligible customers can complete their reporting through the government's free system.

However, its descriptions of potential extension refusal and blacklisting introduce additional reasons for customers to regard professional assistance as necessary.

**The agency combines routine paid filing assistance with warnings about severe immigration consequences that are not adequately substantiated for the reporting violation being discussed.**

This does not establish that the business deliberately exaggerated those consequences to increase sales.

It establishes that the claims appear in promotional material and may influence customers' decisions about purchasing reporting assistance.

### Repeated Violations Do Not Eliminate the Need for Evidence

Repeated reporting failures may legitimately attract additional attention from immigration authorities.

They may also occur alongside unrelated violations, including overstays, false statements, or failure to comply with an immigration order.

Such circumstances can involve consequences more serious than an ordinary TM47 fine.

However, the possibility of additional misconduct does not establish that repeated late reporting alone is a recognized ground for blacklisting.

Thai Visa Centre has not published the legal provision, official instruction, or immigration decision supporting that particular claim.

**The question is not whether serious immigration violations can result in blacklisting. They clearly can. The question is whether TVC has a factual basis for presenting blacklisting as a possible escalation of ordinary TM47 reporting failures.**

The available government materials do not establish that connection.

The existing investigation documents Thai Visa Centre's incorrect instructions for LTR holders, who are generally subject to annual TM95 reporting rather than quarterly TM47 notifications.

## Digital Work Permits Advertised for LTR Applicants Who Are Ineligible to Receive Them

Thai Visa Centre repeatedly advertises digital work permits as a benefit of Thailand's Long-Term Resident (LTR) visa for Work-from-Thailand Professionals.

Its dedicated guide to this category describes the permit as an official document authorizing remote employment for a foreign company while residing in Thailand.[^123]

The article goes further, instructing applicants to obtain a digital work permit after receiving their LTR visa.

However, Thailand's Board of Investment (BOI), which administers the LTR program, explicitly states that **Work-from-Thailand Professionals are not eligible to receive work permits**.[^124][^125]

The discrepancy is not a minor technicality.

**Thai Visa Centre advertises the issuance of a government document that the responsible government agency expressly refuses to issue to applicants in the advertised visa category.**

### The Dedicated Guide Promises Digital Work Permits

Thai Visa Centre's article *LTR Visa Thailand for Work-from-Thailand Professionals: Remote Employer Guide* presents the digital work permit as a central benefit of the category.[^123]

Its introduction describes remote employees of overseas companies as receiving a digital work permit instead of a traditional Thai employment permit.

A summary table identifies the category's work-permit type as **"Digital only."**

The article subsequently states that the Work-from-Thailand category issues a digital work permit authorizing remote employment for an overseas employer.

Under its application instructions, Thai Visa Centre includes a specific fifth step titled **"Digital work permit issuance."**

That step tells successful LTR applicants to apply for the permit through the program after receiving their visa.

The claim is repeated throughout the article:

- The category comparison identifies digital work permits for remote workers.
- The benefits table describes the permit as proof of legal remote employment.
- The comparison against DTV identifies digital work permits as a distinctive LTR advantage.
- The FAQ promotes the category as suitable for professionals who need documented authorization to work remotely.

**The promised work permit is not incidental to the sales presentation. It is repeatedly described as an important reason to select this visa category.**

### The BOI Explicitly Says No Permit Is Issued

The BOI's official LTR visa issuance instructions establish a different procedure.

Under its section concerning work-permit applications, the BOI explains that eligible LTR holders working for entities in Thailand must apply for employment authorization through the LTR system.[^124]

However, the same section contains an explicit exclusion.

**Work-from-Thailand Professionals do not receive work permits because their category is intended for remote employment with companies located outside Thailand.**

The BOI's official FAQ independently reinforces this distinction.[^125]

It specifically asks why Work-from-Thailand Professionals cannot apply for digital work permits.

The answer explains that the category is intended for foreign employees working remotely rather than undertaking local employment.

The FAQ also states that these visa holders cannot undertake income-generating work for Thai employers because the category does not provide work-permit eligibility.

The official distinction is therefore:

| LTR category | Digital work permit |
|---|---|
| Work-from-Thailand Professionals | Not issued |
| Highly Skilled Professionals working for qualifying Thai entities | Available subject to approval |
| Other LTR holders undertaking eligible employment with Thai entities | Subject to applicable work-permit requirements |

The existence of a digital work-permit system for some LTR visa holders does not establish that every category qualifies.

**Thai Visa Centre has applied a benefit available to certain LTR holders to a category that the BOI specifically excludes.**

### Remote Work Permission Is Not the Same as Receiving a Work Permit

An important distinction must be preserved.

The BOI created the Work-from-Thailand Professionals category specifically to accommodate qualifying foreign employees who perform remote work for overseas companies while residing in Thailand.

That activity is permitted within the category's conditions.

The absence of an issued Thai work permit does not automatically make their qualifying remote employment unlawful.

Instead, the category provides an immigration framework designed for that particular arrangement without requiring or issuing the same employment permit used for work involving Thai entities.

This is different from a Highly Skilled Professional employed by a qualifying company or institution in Thailand.

Those applicants may require a digital work permit because their employment involves activities within the Thai labor market.

**The problem with Thai Visa Centre's explanation is not that Work-from-Thailand LTR holders cannot legally work remotely. It is the claim that their authorization is documented through a digital work permit the government will not issue.**

That distinction directly contradicts the central explanation in TVC's guide.

### The Same Error Appears in Other Visa Comparisons

The incorrect claim is not confined to one publication.

Thai Visa Centre's guide *Thai Elite Visa vs LTR Visa* describes the LTR program as providing a **digital permit for remote workers** while presenting a separate work-permit pathway for highly skilled professionals.[^126]

Its application checklist goes further, instructing remote workers to obtain a digital work permit after visa issuance.

That guidance repeats the same procedural error found in the dedicated Work-from-Thailand article.

The agency's *DTV Visa Thailand FAQs* similarly compares DTV, LTR, and Thailand Privilege visas.

Its comparison table describes LTR remote work as permitted **with a digital work permit**.[^127]

The company's primary LTR service page also promotes digital work permits among the program's general benefits and describes a digital permit as required for work activities, without clearly explaining the exclusion affecting Work-from-Thailand Professionals.[^128]

These publications reinforce one another.

A reader researching remote work, comparing long-term visa categories, and reviewing the application process could encounter essentially the same incorrect representation repeatedly.

**Thai Visa Centre presents the unavailable permit as a defining advantage of Work-from-Thailand LTR status across several independently published immigration guides.**

### The Application Instructions Describe an Unavailable Step

The most consequential error concerns the procedure applicants are told to follow.

Thai Visa Centre's dedicated Work-from-Thailand guide presents the following sequence:

1. Establish eligibility through foreign employment and income.
2. Register through the BOI's LTR application system.
3. Obtain qualification endorsement.
4. Receive the LTR visa.
5. Apply for a digital work permit.
6. Maintain post-arrival immigration compliance.

The fifth step is incorrect for this category.

The BOI's published procedure specifically excludes Work-from-Thailand Professionals from work-permit issuance.[^124]

Applicants following TVC's guidance could therefore attempt to complete an unavailable government procedure.

They might also incorrectly assume that receiving a digital permit is necessary before beginning qualifying remote work.

Alternatively, applicants could choose the Work-from-Thailand category partly because they believe it provides a formal employment document that could be presented to banks, employers, insurers, or other institutions.

**Thai Visa Centre advertises both a government benefit and a procedural step that do not exist for the applicants its article is specifically targeting.**

### A Misleading Comparison With Other Immigration Options

The false permit claim also distorts the financial and practical comparison between LTR visas and competing long-stay arrangements.

Work-from-Thailand LTR applicants must satisfy relatively demanding financial and employment criteria.

Thai Visa Centre's guide identifies an annual income threshold generally beginning at US$80,000, with alternative qualifications for some applicants earning less.

The digital work permit is presented as an additional reason to satisfy those requirements rather than choose the less expensive Destination Thailand Visa.

That distinction is commercially significant.

Customers might reasonably value an official work-permit document when comparing long-term immigration options.

However, **a benefit that cannot be issued should not be used to justify choosing one visa over another**.

The BOI does charge separate digital work-permit fees for eligible LTR applicants working in Thailand, including a published maintenance fee of 3,000 baht annually.[^124]

Those charges concern the work-permit process available to eligible applicants, not an additional permit that Work-from-Thailand Professionals are required or entitled to obtain.

The available evidence does not establish that TVC has charged remote workers for nonexistent work permits.

It does establish that its public advertising inaccurately identifies a government-issued document as part of their expected immigration process.

### Claimed Professional Expertise Versus Official Instructions

Thai Visa Centre's Work-from-Thailand guide repeatedly encourages readers to consult its Bangkok team before submitting applications.

The company claims to assess overseas employers, financial documentation, income qualifications, and work-permit requirements against BOI rules.

It also lists the BOI's official LTR portal among its supporting references.

Yet the BOI's published instructions explicitly contradict the guidance.

This is particularly significant because the agency markets its expertise as a way to avoid applications based on incorrect eligibility assumptions.

A professional adviser handling LTR cases should distinguish between permission to work remotely under an approved visa category and eligibility for a separate Thai employment permit.

**Thai Visa Centre repeatedly fails to make that distinction while claiming expertise in the very government program whose official instructions contradict its advice.**

### The Missing Explanation

The company should explain why its publications describe digital work-permit issuance for Work-from-Thailand Professionals when the BOI expressly excludes those applicants.

It should also correct the application sequence, clarify which LTR categories may apply for digital permits, and identify whether customers have been advised to pursue or pay for unavailable work-permit processing.

The evidence does not establish that TVC has fabricated work permits, submitted unauthorized applications, or caused a particular customer financial loss.

Those outcomes would require additional documentary evidence.

**What is established is that Thai Visa Centre repeatedly advertises an official employment document that the responsible government authority states will not be issued for the visa category being promoted.**

Unlike a disputed customer testimonial or an unsupported accreditation claim, this discrepancy can be resolved directly through the government's published eligibility rules.

It adds another substantial inconsistency to the existing investigation into Thai Visa Centre's LTR advertising, immigration advice, and representations about professional processing expertise.

**An agency claiming to specialize in complex immigration applications should not direct customers toward a work permit that Thailand's own investment authority explicitly states they cannot obtain.**

Different data-refresh schedules might explain small discrepancies between the two publications.

They do not explain why the main archive omits one-star reviews or leaves hundreds of advertised ratings without a visible classification.

## Mandatory LTR Health Insurance Claims Contradict Official BOI Alternatives

Thai Visa Centre publishes misleading instructions concerning the health-insurance requirements of Thailand's Long-Term Resident (LTR) visa program.

Its principal LTR sales page identifies health insurance as a required application document and states that visa holders must maintain valid health insurance throughout their stay.[^131]

However, Thailand's Board of Investment (BOI), which administers the LTR program, explicitly permits eligible applicants to satisfy the corresponding financial and healthcare requirement through alternatives to private health insurance.[^132]

Those alternatives include qualifying Thai social security coverage and bank deposits maintained for at least 12 months.

**Thai Visa Centre presents private health insurance as mandatory even though the responsible government authority explicitly permits applicants to qualify without purchasing an insurance policy.**

The error also affects dependents, whose official requirements include a separate, lower bank-deposit alternative.

### Health Insurance Presented as a Universal Requirement

Thai Visa Centre's dedicated *Long-Term Resident Visa (LTR)* page advertises immigration assistance across the program's four principal categories.

Its category-specific documentation lists repeatedly identify health insurance with minimum coverage of US$50,000 as a required document.[^131]

The same page includes a separate section titled *Health Insurance*, instructing applicants to provide a policy meeting that coverage threshold for their stay in Thailand.

Under *Restrictions*, the website makes an even more categorical claim:

> Must maintain valid health insurance

This instruction appears alongside legitimate continuing obligations, including maintaining qualification criteria, completing annual immigration reporting, and complying with employment requirements.

The page does not explain that the insurance requirement can be satisfied through other forms of evidence.

**A prospective applicant reading TVC's service description would reasonably conclude that obtaining and maintaining private health insurance is a non-negotiable condition of LTR approval.**

That conclusion conflicts with the government's published requirements.

### The BOI Explicitly Permits Three Alternatives

The BOI's official LTR program website identifies three acceptable methods of satisfying the healthcare-related requirement for principal applicants.[^132]

| Qualification pathway | Official requirement |
|---|---|
| Private or qualifying group health insurance | At least US$50,000 in medical coverage |
| Thai social security | Valid benefits covering hospitalization and medical treatment in Thailand |
| Bank-deposit alternative | At least US$100,000 maintained for a minimum of 12 months |

These are alternatives rather than cumulative requirements.

Applicants do not automatically need private health insurance if they provide qualifying social security evidence or satisfy the bank-deposit option.

The BOI repeats this arrangement across its descriptions of Wealthy Global Citizens, Wealthy Pensioners, Work-from-Thailand Professionals, and Highly Skilled Professionals.

The agency's official document checklist for Work-from-Thailand Professionals independently confirms the alternatives.[^133]

That checklist specifies that qualifying bank evidence must demonstrate a balance of at least US$100,000 maintained for at least 12 months.

It also clarifies that investment portfolios and fixed deposits are not accepted for that particular form of evidence.

**The BOI does not impose a universal requirement to purchase private health insurance. It requires applicants to demonstrate compliance through one of several accepted pathways.**

### The Official Rules for Dependents Are Different

The distinction is equally important for spouses and children applying as LTR dependents.

The BOI's published dependent requirements allow applicants to satisfy the healthcare-related condition through:

- Qualifying insurance providing at least US$50,000 in coverage.
- Valid Thai social security benefits covering hospitalization and medical treatment.
- A qualifying bank deposit of at least US$25,000 maintained for a minimum of 12 months.

The BOI's November 6, 2025 document checklist expressly identifies these three alternatives.[^134]

The US$25,000 threshold is substantially lower than the US$100,000 bank-deposit alternative applicable to principal applicants.

However, Thai Visa Centre's insurance FAQ states that each dependent carries their own policy.[^135]

That wording again suggests private insurance is mandatory for every family member.

**A dependent satisfying the BOI's US$25,000 deposit requirement does not automatically need to purchase a separate private health-insurance policy merely because they are applying under the LTR program.**

The eligibility of any particular dependent remains subject to the BOI's documentary requirements and assessment.

Nevertheless, the government's published rules expressly permit alternatives that TVC's categorical instructions fail to describe.

### Thai Visa Centre's Own Insurance FAQ Acknowledges the Alternative

The inconsistency is particularly revealing because Thai Visa Centre has published the correct distinction elsewhere on its website.

Its FAQ *Do I Need Health Insurance for Thailand?* discusses insurance requirements across different immigration categories.[^135]

Under its LTR section, the article acknowledges that applicants may use an equivalent cash-deposit alternative under BOI rules.

That acknowledgment directly undermines the main LTR sales page's statement that valid health insurance must be maintained.

The company's separate guide *How to Stay in Thailand for 20 Years* introduces the same confusion.[^136]

Its planning checklist states that health insurance is mandatory for O-A retirement visas, required for LTR, and recommended for Thailand Privilege.

The guide repeats that classification without explaining the BOI's alternatives.

The result is three materially different presentations:

1. **Main LTR sales page:** Health insurance is required and must be maintained.
2. **Twenty-year residence guide:** Health insurance is required for LTR applicants.
3. **Insurance FAQ:** A cash-deposit alternative exists under BOI rules.

**Thai Visa Centre already acknowledges that the LTR insurance requirement has exceptions, yet continues advertising private insurance as compulsory in its main commercial guidance.**

The company does not explain why prospective customers receive different information depending on which article they consult.

### Health Insurance and Financial Evidence Are Not Interchangeable Costs

The BOI's alternatives have different financial implications.

Purchasing private health insurance generally involves paying premiums to an insurer.

A qualifying bank deposit instead involves maintaining a substantial balance that remains the applicant's own money, subject to the government's requirements.

These are materially different financial commitments.

A bank deposit is not the same as an application fee or an insurance premium.

However, maintaining US$100,000 in qualifying funds can impose a substantial liquidity requirement and may be impractical for applicants who would otherwise prefer purchasing insurance.

Likewise, Thai social security coverage is available only where applicants can demonstrate valid qualifying benefits.

**The alternatives are not necessarily cheaper or more convenient for every applicant, but their existence is central to determining which LTR application strategy is appropriate.**

A professional visa adviser should explain those choices rather than presenting one option as universally mandatory.

### The Error Can Affect Families and Older Applicants

The omission may be particularly consequential for applicants who face difficulties obtaining private health insurance.

Some older applicants may encounter high premiums, exclusions, or difficulty obtaining coverage for pre-existing medical conditions.

Other applicants may already possess qualifying financial resources or valid Thai social security benefits.

For these individuals, the BOI's alternatives may materially affect whether an LTR application is practical.

A family applying for LTR status may also need to compare the cost of multiple insurance policies with the separate financial-evidence requirements for dependents.

**Telling every applicant and dependent that private health insurance is mandatory can cause unnecessary concern, discourage otherwise eligible applicants, or lead customers to purchase coverage that the government does not require under their chosen qualification pathway.**

The public evidence does not establish that Thai Visa Centre has sold unnecessary insurance policies, received insurance commissions, or charged customers for avoidable documentation.

Those conclusions would require transaction records or customer testimony.

The documented problem is the agency's inaccurate explanation of the government's eligibility requirements.

### Maintaining Qualification Does Not Necessarily Mean Maintaining Insurance

The BOI requires LTR visa holders to maintain the conditions under which their applications were approved.

These continuing obligations may include employment arrangements, investment amounts, bank balances, and insurance coverage, depending on the applicant's specific qualification pathway.[^132]

That requirement does not establish that everyone must maintain private insurance.

An applicant approved through the bank-deposit alternative must satisfy the applicable financial conditions.

An applicant relying on qualifying insurance must maintain the required coverage.

An applicant relying on Thai social security must satisfy the corresponding eligibility requirements.

**Thai Visa Centre's blanket instruction to maintain valid health insurance fails to distinguish between the different methods of satisfying the BOI's underlying requirement.**

The company should explain which continuing obligations apply to each pathway instead of describing one option as universal.

### The Broader Pattern of LTR Misinformation

The existing investigation has documented two other important discrepancies in Thai Visa Centre's LTR guidance.

First, the agency advertises digital work permits for Work-from-Thailand Professionals despite the BOI explicitly excluding that category from work-permit issuance.

Second, its 90-day reporting guide incorrectly tells LTR holders to submit quarterly TM47 notifications even though the government replaces that procedure with annual TM95 reporting.

The health-insurance discrepancy adds a third independent problem.

All three concern material benefits, obligations, or eligibility conditions associated with the same government immigration program.

They also share a common characteristic: **the correct information is available through official BOI publications, and some of it is acknowledged elsewhere on TVC's own website**.

This raises questions about whether the agency's published immigration guidance is reviewed consistently against the primary government requirements it claims to explain.

### Questions the Company Should Answer

Thai Visa Centre should clarify why its main LTR sales page requires private health insurance despite the BOI's officially permitted alternatives.

It should also:

1. Identify the US$100,000 bank-deposit alternative for principal applicants.
2. Explain the qualifying Thai social security pathway.
3. Identify the US$25,000 deposit alternative available to eligible dependents.
4. Correct categorical statements requiring all LTR holders to maintain insurance.
5. Distinguish bank-balance maintenance from insurance-premium payments.
6. Reconcile its main sales page, insurance FAQ, and long-term residence guides.

The company should further clarify whether its professional document-preparation service advises applicants about all accepted qualification pathways before recommending particular insurance arrangements.

The available evidence does not establish intentional deception, unnecessary insurance sales, or financial harm to a specific customer.

**It does establish that Thai Visa Centre repeatedly presents one optional method of satisfying the BOI's healthcare-related requirements as though it were mandatory for all LTR applicants.**

This is a consequential factual error because the government expressly provides alternatives for applicants who meet the relevant criteria.

**An immigration agency advertising expertise in the LTR program should not tell customers they must purchase or maintain private health insurance when the responsible government authority explicitly permits approval through qualifying social security benefits or maintained bank deposits.**

## Categorical T8 Health-Form Advice Omits Continuing Disease-Control Requirements

Thai Visa Centre publishes categorical statements declaring Thailand's T8 health declaration discontinued.

Its dedicated guide *Health Declaration T8 Form: What Happened to It?* tells travelers that the form is no longer required, describes it primarily as a COVID-era document, and instructs visitors not to download or print it.[^137]

A separate travel-planning guide repeats that T8 was a pandemic measure replaced by Thailand's Digital Arrival Card (TDAC) for all arrivals.[^138]

These statements correctly reflect the removal of routine paper health declarations for ordinary travelers.

However, they omit a consequential distinction.

**Thailand continues to impose special health-declaration, vaccination, and disease-control requirements on travelers arriving from certain countries. Official 2026 guidance also warns that some affected passengers may still need a separate T8 declaration before boarding.**

The discrepancy matters because an immigration agency advising international travelers must distinguish the removal of routine paperwork from the continuing requirements applicable to travelers with specific epidemiological risks.

### Thai Visa Centre Declares T8 Discontinued

Thai Visa Centre's dedicated T8 guide opens with a categorical explanation:

> Here is the straight answer for 2026: the T8 is no longer required.

The article identifies the form's status as **"Discontinued"** and describes it as a paper pandemic document no longer used at the border.[^137]

Its FAQ repeats that travelers do not need to download T8 because the form has been discontinued.

The guide additionally characterizes the T8 primarily as part of Thailand's COVID-19 border-control arrangements between approximately 2020 and 2022.

Its separate article *20 Essential Tips Before Traveling to Thailand* gives substantially the same answer.

That publication states that the paper T8 declaration was a pandemic measure replaced by TDAC for all arrivals.[^138]

**The combined effect is to present T8 as obsolete paperwork whose relevance ended with Thailand's pandemic entry restrictions.**

That explanation is incomplete.

### T8 Has a Separate Yellow-Fever History

Thailand's T8 health declaration has also been used for screening travelers arriving from yellow-fever-affected countries.

It was not exclusively a COVID-19 form.

The Thai Health Pass platform identifies the declaration as a health-screening process associated with Thailand's Department of Disease Control.[^139]

The form's historical applications include the reporting of travel history and health information for visitors from designated disease-control areas.

This distinction is important because the removal of pandemic-wide requirements does not necessarily eliminate disease-specific health controls.

Thailand continues identifying countries and territories subject to yellow-fever vaccination and screening requirements.

The government's 2026 international-meeting travel guidance explicitly confirms that travelers arriving from designated yellow-fever-affected countries must present an International Health Certificate establishing qualifying vaccination.[^141]

The certificate must be presented to the relevant health-control and immigration authorities.

**The fact that ordinary arrivals no longer complete a standalone T8 paper form does not establish that every traveler has been released from special health-documentation requirements.**

### T8 Registration Was Integrated Into TDAC

The present position is more nuanced than either a universal requirement or complete disappearance.

The Thai Health Pass website now explains that T8 health-declaration registration has moved into the Thailand Digital Arrival Card system.[^139]

The official Immigration Bureau TDAC guidance likewise describes health declarations as part of the arrival-card process.[^140]

It explains that travelers may be asked for additional health information according to their nationality, recent travel history, departure location, or flight.

In other words, the TDAC system does not simply collect passport and accommodation details.

It also serves public-health screening purposes.

| Issue | Published official position |
|---|---|
| Routine paper arrival card | Replaced by TDAC |
| Ordinary T8 health declaration | Incorporated into digital arrival procedures |
| Health questions for designated travelers | May be required through TDAC |
| Yellow-fever vaccination certification | Continues for qualifying travelers |
| Separate T8 requested by an airline | May still apply in specified circumstances |

**The government's current system incorporates health declarations into TDAC rather than abolishing all health-screening obligations formerly associated with T8.**

Thai Visa Centre's general explanation of TDAC does not adequately identify this distinction.

### November 2026 Guidance Warns That Separate T8 Forms May Still Be Requested

The discrepancy becomes more significant when compared with contemporary travel instructions.

A United Nations information note dated November 4, 2026, prepared for participants traveling to Thailand, confirms the continued application of special health-control measures.[^142]

Its health-control section explains that travelers from designated yellow-fever-affected countries must present valid vaccination documentation.

It also describes additional screening measures for travelers arriving from specified Ebola-affected areas.

Crucially, the publication warns:

> Travellers from Yellow Fever and Ebola affected countries may also be required by the airline to present a separate completed Health Declaration Form T.8 form before boarding.

This is not a statement that every traveler must complete T8.

It is a conditional warning for passengers with particular travel histories.

Nevertheless, **the warning directly undermines an unqualified instruction that T8 has been completely discontinued and should never be prepared for a 2026 journey**.

The distinction matters at check-in, where airlines may verify health documents before permitting a passenger to board.

A traveler relying on TVC's categorical explanation could fail to investigate additional documentation required for their itinerary.

### Ebola Controls Demonstrate the Continuing Risk

Thailand's 2026 health-control requirements extend beyond yellow fever.

Government-published guidance for international visitors identifies special Ebola screening and quarantine arrangements affecting travelers arriving from designated countries, including the Democratic Republic of the Congo.[^141]

The November 2026 United Nations travel instructions separately identify restrictions affecting travelers arriving from or through specified Ebola-affected areas.[^142]

These measures are distinct from the former general COVID-19 quarantine rules.

They reflect disease-specific public-health powers and can change as outbreak conditions develop.

Thai Visa Centre's T8 guide acknowledges in general terms that Thailand may introduce additional screening during outbreaks.

However, it does not identify the existing yellow-fever requirements or the Ebola-related measures relevant during 2026.

**The article presents disease-specific controls as something that might return in the future without adequately explaining the health restrictions already relevant to certain travelers.**

That omission is particularly important because the consequences may include additional screening, mandatory observation, quarantine, or boarding-document requirements.

### The Misleading Historical Explanation

Thai Visa Centre's account also compresses two different historical developments into one narrative.

The first was Thailand's use of health declarations and disease-control documentation for travelers from designated infection-risk areas.

The second was the broader use of health questionnaires and exceptional entry paperwork during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Although both involved health screening, they were not identical programs.

Ending the pandemic's general entry restrictions did not automatically repeal every earlier disease-control requirement.

The Thai Health Pass platform's continuing reference to T8 health declarations demonstrates that the form's terminology and public-health purpose extend beyond the pandemic.[^139]

The government's continued yellow-fever screening requirements independently establish that the underlying disease-control obligations remain relevant.[^141]

**Describing T8 solely as an obsolete COVID-19 form obscures its separate role in Thailand's longstanding infectious-disease screening arrangements.**

### The Correct Advice Depends on Travel History

For most ordinary arrivals, Thai Visa Centre's practical conclusion is broadly correct.

Travelers generally complete TDAC online rather than obtaining a separate paper T8 declaration.

However, a traveler arriving from a designated yellow-fever area or another country subject to special disease-control measures should verify additional requirements.

Those requirements may involve:

1. Completing health declarations through TDAC.
2. Presenting a qualifying international vaccination certificate.
3. Undergoing screening at a port-health checkpoint.
4. Complying with additional outbreak-specific public-health instructions.
5. Providing a separate T8 declaration when requested by the airline or competent authority.

Not all five requirements apply to every affected traveler.

The applicable conditions depend on the disease, recent travel history, public-health notices, and current instructions from the authorities and carrier.

**The critical distinction is between ordinary immigration arrival paperwork and special public-health obligations that may remain applicable independently of ordinary TDAC registration.**

A professional travel adviser should explain that distinction rather than simply declaring the older form irrelevant.

### The Importance of Accurate Arrival Advice

Thai Visa Centre markets professional assistance with visas, entry documentation, and TDAC submissions.

Its T8 guide specifically encourages travelers to seek its help with current arrival procedures.

Customers may therefore encounter the company's explanations while preparing documentation for an international flight.

For travelers arriving from countries subject to yellow-fever or Ebola-related measures, incomplete health-documentation advice can have consequences beyond administrative inconvenience.

Depending on the applicable requirements, travelers may face boarding difficulties, additional health checks, or other restrictions imposed by public-health authorities.

The available evidence does not establish that a traveler has been refused boarding or entry because of Thai Visa Centre's published guidance.

It also does not establish that a separate paper T8 form remains universally mandatory.

**What is established is that Thai Visa Centre presents the form as categorically discontinued while contemporary official guidance retains circumstances in which additional health declarations, including a possible separate T8 request, remain relevant.**

### Questions the Agency Should Answer

Thai Visa Centre should clarify whether its statement that T8 is discontinued applies only to ordinary arrivals.

It should also distinguish the electronic integration of T8 health declarations into TDAC from continuing requirements for designated infection-risk travelers.

Its travel guides should explain the current yellow-fever vaccination requirements and direct travelers with relevant itineraries to official outbreak-specific instructions.

The company should further acknowledge that airlines may impose additional document checks for travelers arriving from affected countries, as identified in the November 2026 United Nations guidance.

These corrections would not require treating T8 as universally mandatory again.

They would simply distinguish ordinary procedures from the exceptional requirements still applicable to some passengers.

**Thai Visa Centre tells travelers that T8 is discontinued while failing to explain continuing public-health requirements under which travelers from specified countries may still be asked to provide a separate health declaration.**

The discrepancy adds another example of incomplete immigration and entry guidance from a company advertising specialist knowledge of Thailand's changing travel requirements.

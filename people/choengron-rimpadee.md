# Choengron Rimpadee

Police Major General Choengron Rimpadee (เชิงรณ ริมผดี) is a senior Royal Thai Police officer who commanded Immigration Division 2 at Suvarnabhumi Airport during the 2025 rollout of Thailand's Digital Arrival Card (TDAC) system before becoming deputy commissioner and spokesperson for the Immigration Bureau. On March 29, 2026, he publicly warned that at least 10% of foreign arrivals had registered through fraudulent TDAC websites that were not connected to the government's immigration system, with some charging travelers for a service that is officially free. Rimpadee directed travelers to the legitimate government portal, `tdac.immigration.go.th`, warned that personal information submitted to fraudulent websites could be misused, and confirmed that immigration authorities were coordinating efforts to shut down these operations and pursue legal action.[^1][^2]


## March 2024: Paid Airport Fast-Track Warnings and Unresolved Enforcement

The problem of paid airport privileges had already exposed misconduct within the Royal Thai Police. In January 2023, authorities investigated four officers accused of providing paid escort services to Chinese tourists arriving at Suvarnabhumi Airport. Investigators found that officers had used private vehicles fitted with unauthorized sirens and police-style markings. All four were removed from their regular duties and transferred pending disciplinary and criminal proceedings. The incident predated Choengron Rimpadee's subsequent warnings and does not establish his personal involvement.[^15]

On March 27, 2024, Rimpadee, then commander of Immigration Division 2 and an Immigration Bureau spokesperson, publicly identified a Facebook page called `richcarsbangkok` advertising airport immigration fast-track services for 2,900 baht per flight. He stated that no private company was authorized to sell access to immigration fast-track lanes at Suvarnabhumi or Don Mueang airports. Special immigration channels, he explained, were restricted to eligible passenger categories rather than customers paying private agents.[^14]

Rimpadee said authorities had investigated the advertisement, determined its claims were false, and were examining other businesses promoting similar services with a view to taking legal action. His warning was published on an official Immigration Division 2 website, demonstrating that authorities were aware of the commercial advertising and had identified at least one operator by name.[^14]

Yet comparable claims continued appearing in later years. In March 2026, Thai Visa Service advertised paid airport "safe entry" packages costing between 2,000 and 5,500 baht, while a June 2026 investigation concerned brokers allegedly charging 4,500–12,000 baht for guaranteed entry and possible assistance from immigration officers. These services were not necessarily identical to the fast-track product Rimpadee condemned, but they demonstrate the persistence of businesses marketing privileged access to immigration procedures.[^9][^11]

The available reports do not establish what legal consequences, if any, followed the investigation of `richcarsbangkok`, or whether similar advertisers faced consistent enforcement. Given the earlier misconduct involving serving police officers, assurances that unauthorized fast-track services do not officially exist are insufficient to establish that such arrangements cannot occur. The repeated cycle of public warnings, promised investigations, and continuing advertisements raises legitimate questions about enforcement effectiveness, transparency, and the Immigration Bureau's willingness to confront possible internal complicity.

## April 2025: Launch of Thailand's Digital Arrival Card

In April 2025, Choengron Rimpadee, then commander of Immigration Division 2 at Suvarnabhumi Airport, publicly explained Thailand's new Digital Arrival Card (TDAC) system ahead of its nationwide launch on May 1. The system replaced paper arrival cards with mandatory online registration for foreign travelers entering Thailand by air, land, or sea. Registration opened on April 28 for travelers arriving from May 1 onward.[^1][^4]

Rimpadee explained that TDAC registrations would be integrated with the Immigration Bureau's biometric database, allowing authorities to screen arriving foreigners against criminal records and identify potential transnational security threats. He also stated that travelers' declared accommodation details would automatically enter immigration databases, replacing portions of the previous manual data-entry process.[^1]

According to Rimpadee, the new system was intended to improve immigration screening while making arrivals more convenient for travelers. Its integration with official immigration databases would later become an important distinction in his March 2026 warnings about fraudulent third-party TDAC websites that were not connected to government systems.[^1][^2]

## March 2026: Warning About Fraudulent TDAC Websites

On March 29, 2026, Choengron Rimpadee warned that unofficial Thailand Digital Arrival Card (TDAC) websites were collecting travelers' personal information and charging unnecessary registration fees. He explained that these websites were not connected to the Immigration Bureau's registration system, meaning travelers could pay for a submission that did not reach immigration authorities.[^2][^3]

Public reporting of his warning identified websites including `tdac.info`, `ivisa.com/thailand/digital-arrival-card`, `thailandarrivalcardtourist.com`, `thailandarrivalcard2025.com`, `thailandonarrivalweb.com`, and `australiaasiagroup.com/thailand`. These were presented as examples of unofficial or paid TDAC services, although their inclusion did not establish criminal wrongdoing by every operator.[^3]

Rimpadee also warned that personal information collected through fraudulent registration websites could be exploited by scammers. He said the Immigration Bureau was coordinating with other government agencies to identify and shut down unlawful operations, but enforcement was complicated by websites operating outside Thailand. Immigration authorities had also sought assistance from Thai consular offices and international airlines to warn travelers about fraudulent registration services and direct them to the official government portal.[^2][^3]

## 2026: Omission of Thai Visa Centre's TDAC Websites From Fraud Warnings

On March 29, 2026, Choengron Rimpadee publicly warned about fraudulent Thailand Digital Arrival Card websites, identifying numerous third-party operators and announcing efforts to shut down unlawful services. However, the published warnings conspicuously omitted `tdac.in.th` and `tdac.agents.co.th`, despite Thai Visa Centre having publicly acknowledged its connection to both websites nearly a year earlier.[^3][^5]

In May 2025, the official ThaiVisaCentre account on ASEAN NOW explicitly identified both domains as operated by AGENTS CO., LTD. The agency also advertised an $8 paid early-submission service through `tdac.in.th`. As of late 2026, the website continued directing visitors to an AGENTS-operated platform advertising paid TDAC submissions while simultaneously publishing warnings that characterized competing third-party services as scams.[^5][^6]

The omission is particularly striking because Thailand's Immigration Bureau had already issued broader warnings against unofficial registration websites. In January 2026, Thailand's Anti-Fake News Center published an Immigration Bureau clarification identifying `tdac.immigration.go.th` as the only official registration portal and describing other websites offering comparable registration services as unaffiliated and fake.[^7]

Despite Rimpadee's prominent role in implementing TDAC and his subsequent public statements about enforcement against fraudulent websites, the reviewed reports provide no explanation for the exclusion of these Thai-operated domains or documentation of enforcement action against their operators. The continued promotion of these services raises serious questions about the consistency of immigration authorities' warnings and enforcement priorities, particularly when other commercial TDAC websites were publicly identified by name.

## 2025–2026: Airport Entry Brokers Claiming Immigration Connections

On June 9, 2025, Choengron Rimpadee, then commander of Immigration Division 2 at Suvarnabhumi Airport, responded to a viral video suggesting that travelers could obtain preferential immigration treatment through payments or personal connections. Rimpadee disputed the allegations, explaining that the immigration officer shown had assisted a Chinese traveler with TDAC registration before directing him through normal passport control procedures.[^8]

More broadly, Rimpadee warned that certain individuals and visa agents were using misleading advertisements and social media content to claim they could arrange entry into Thailand through connections with immigration officials. He also reported that immigration officers had refused entry to approximately 8,193 foreigners between January and May 2025 amid intensified screening of travelers suspected of abusing visa-free entry privileges.[^8]

Commercial services promising special airport treatment nevertheless remained widely advertised. In March 2026, Thai Visa Service published a brochure explicitly guaranteeing entry through Suvarnabhumi and Don Mueang airports, advertising prices ranging from 2,000 to 5,500 baht and claiming to check travelers' status directly with immigration before arrival. The company distinguished its paid "safe entry" service from ordinary airport fast-track assistance.[^9] Separate online discussions described similar VIP airport services offered by Thai Visa Centre, including alleged immigration escorts and guarantees of admission, although those accounts remain unverified.[^10]

On June 23, 2026, Immigration Division 2, by then under a different commander, announced an investigation into alleged Chinese-linked "bao guan" brokers demanding between 4,500 and 12,000 baht for supposedly guaranteed entry into Thailand. Authorities established a fact-finding committee to investigate the brokers and determine whether immigration officers had participated in the arrangements. No finding of official involvement was established by the announcement.[^11]

These incidents illustrate a broader commercial market built around claims of privileged airport access, immigration connections, and guaranteed admission. Rimpadee's earlier warnings directly addressed this type of marketing, but the available public record does not explain which private arrangements immigration authorities considered legitimate, how they investigated individual providers, or whether similar claims were consistently scrutinized across different agencies.

## September 2025: Airport Trafficking Allegations and the Immigration Bureau's Response

On September 18, 2025, Reuters published an investigation based on interviews with nine people trafficked through Thailand to cyber-scam compounds in Myanmar between 2022 and 2025. Six said individuals they believed to be immigration officials escorted them through Bangkok's international airports before they were transported to Myanmar. Kenya's ambassador to Thailand, Lindsay Kiptiness, also told Reuters that rescued trafficking victims had repeatedly described being assisted through Thai airports by officials. He said he had provided Thai authorities with information about the allegations but received no follow-up. Reuters could not independently establish the identities of the alleged officials or confirm their involvement.[^12]

On September 20, Police Major General Choengron Rimpadee, then commander of Immigration Division 2, publicly rejected the investigation, describing it as "essentially fictional." He disputed the account of one victim identified as "Oly," citing a search of immigration records and restrictions on access to airport baggage-claim areas. He also highlighted tighter screening procedures introduced in January 2025 and reported that 16,538 travelers had been denied entry since then.[^13]

These responses challenged elements of one account but did not publicly resolve the broader allegations involving six witnesses or the Kenyan ambassador's claim that authorities had failed to follow up. The search for records matching "Oly" was also not conclusive on its own: Reuters identified the man by his first name and said he had traveled from Ethiopia, without establishing that he was an Ethiopian national.[^12][^13]

The central accountability question is whether the Immigration Bureau investigated possible misconduct by its own personnel with the same urgency it devoted to rejecting the reporting. The public responses reviewed did not present findings from an independent inquiry into the six accounts, a comprehensive examination of airport surveillance and access records, or disciplinary outcomes. This does not establish that immigration officers participated in trafficking, or that Choengron was personally involved. It does, however, expose a significant gap between categorical official denials and publicly demonstrated scrutiny of serious corruption allegations.

## October 2025: KK Park Exodus and Immigration Screening

In October 2025, more than 1,000 foreign nationals fled Myanmar's notorious KK Park scam compound into Thailand following a military operation against the facility. Those crossing into Tak province included nationals of China, India, Vietnam, Ethiopia, Pakistan, Indonesia, and other countries. Thai authorities began screening the arrivals to identify suspected criminal participants and potential trafficking victims.[^16][^17]

Police Major General Choengron Rimpadee, then spokesperson for the Immigration Bureau, announced that immigration officers were coordinating with security forces to verify identities, examine travel histories, and check criminal records using mobile biometric equipment. He emphasized that immigration laws would be strictly enforced and that individuals suspected of criminal activity would undergo further investigation.[^17]

The incident occurred approximately one month after Reuters reported allegations that trafficking victims had been escorted through Bangkok airports by people believed to be immigration officials before being transported to KK Park. Rimpadee had publicly rejected those allegations, describing the reporting as false and distorted.[^12]

Although the Immigration Bureau detailed its screening procedures for those fleeing KK Park, the reviewed announcements did not disclose whether authorities investigated how those individuals originally traveled through Thailand, identified the brokers who facilitated their movements, or examined possible involvement by immigration personnel. The absence of publicly reported findings leaves questions about the effectiveness of earlier screening measures and the extent of investigations into trafficking networks operating through Thailand.

## November 2025: Visa-Run Crackdown and Warnings to Visa Agencies

In November 2025, Thailand's Immigration Bureau introduced stricter measures against foreigners repeatedly using visa exemptions to remain in the country. The policy targeted travelers making more than two visa runs without adequate justification, individuals flagged at high-risk border crossings, suspicious extensions of stay, and visa overstayers. The government reported that approximately 2,900 foreigners had already been refused entry during 2025 for similar travel patterns.[^18]

On November 24, Police Major General Choengron Rimpadee, then deputy commissioner and spokesperson for the Immigration Bureau, accused commercial visa-run operators of spreading misleading information about the crackdown. He rejected claims that immigration officers were specifically targeting Chinese, Russian, South Korean, or Singaporean visitors, insisting that enforcement focused on repeated long stays and suspected misuse of tourist privileges regardless of nationality.[^19]

Rimpadee described cases involving foreigners entering Thailand more than seven times and spending over 200 days in the country while claiming to be tourists. He alleged that some were working illegally, operating businesses through Thai nominees, or exploiting visa exemptions to avoid proper immigration procedures. Officers were instructed to scrutinize travel histories, accommodation, return tickets, and stated purposes of entry.[^19]

Immigration authorities in Phuket also confirmed that visa agencies had been formally warned against facilitating abusive visa runs. Officials threatened legal action against both travelers and companies found violating the new measures.[^20]

Although the Immigration Bureau published statistics on refused travelers and announced penalties for visa agencies, the reviewed reports did not identify specific agencies prosecuted under the crackdown or provide outcomes of investigations into commercial organizers. Rimpadee's accusations of deliberate misinformation by visa-run operators likewise did not publicly identify the businesses responsible. The announcements therefore document substantial enforcement against individual travelers but provide less evidence of corresponding action against the agencies allegedly facilitating immigration abuse.

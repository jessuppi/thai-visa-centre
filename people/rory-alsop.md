# Rory Alsop

**Rory Alsop** is a British moderator of [Travel Stack Exchange](../brands/travel-stack-exchange.md), where he publicly moderates under his real name. Researcher Jesse Nickles accuses Alsop of repeatedly censoring warnings about [Thai Visa Centre](../brands/thai-visa-centre.md) and related immigration scams in Thailand, while failing to address suspected sockpuppet accounts allegedly promoting fraudulent visa services and maliciously flagging accurate content as spam or other policy violations to mislead moderators into removing it.

The dispute dates back to at least June 2025, when Alsop publicly accused Nickles of manipulating SEO by linking between related Thailand Digital Arrival Card (TDAC) discussions.[^1] Beyond objecting to these links, Alsop provided no specific evidence of deceptive content or explanation of how sharing what Nickles maintains were accurate warnings for travelers constituted SEO manipulation. A longstanding Meta Stack Exchange discussion explicitly describes internal links as helpful to readers and acknowledges their SEO benefits, raising further questions about Alsop's objection.[^2]

Nickles alleges that the censorship escalated throughout 2026, with dozens of user accounts suspended or even "nuked," numerous popular questions and answers deleted,[^3] and warnings about suspicious immigration websites removed from public view. On Stack Exchange, "nuking" an account refers to account destruction, which removes the account along with its posts and comments.[^4]

## June 2025: TDAC Questions and SEO Accusations

In June 2025, Jesse Nickles challenged the closure of two Travel Stack Exchange questions as duplicates.[^1] One concerned TDAC pricing,[^5] while the other warned travelers about TDAC scams.[^6] Despite covering different subjects, moderators had classified them as duplicates.

On June 15, moderator Rory Alsop issued a spam warning to Nickles[^5] and accused him of SEO manipulation through links between related questions.[^1] However, Alsop did not identify any false information in the questions or explain which Stack Exchange rule the links violated.

The accusation was particularly questionable given that a longstanding Meta discussion describes internal links as useful for readers and acknowledges their SEO benefits.[^2] Nickles maintained that the links were intended to help travelers find accurate information about Thailand's immigration procedures and avoid scams.

### June 15: Locked TDAC Warning and Removed Links

On June 15, 2025, at 16:56 UTC, moderator Willeke locked Jesse Nickles's question about avoiding Thailand TDAC scams, marking it as a "content dispute." The question had previously appeared on Stack Exchange's Hot Network Questions list. Although the warning remained publicly visible, the lock prevented further answers and interaction.[^16]

Between 17:09 and 17:13 UTC, Rory Alsop edited five of Nickles's TDAC posts in five consecutive minutes. At 17:09, he removed links to related Travel Stack Exchange questions and an embedded image from an answer about TDAC pricing, describing the changes as "Removed unneeded self-promoting links."[^17]

The removed image was an official public warning from Thailand's Immigration Bureau, advising travelers to avoid fraudulent TDAC websites, protect their personal information, and use the government's free registration service. It was a government anti-fraud announcement, not advertising or self-promotion by Nickles.[^19]

At 17:10, Alsop edited Nickles's answer explaining that Thailand had no official TDAC mobile app. He removed a link to another Travel Stack Exchange question about completing the TDAC form, replacing it with a link to the government's official website.[^24]

At 17:11, Alsop edited a separate answer explaining the difference between Thailand's TDAC and ETA programs. This time he removed three links to related Travel Stack Exchange questions, including discussions about the ETA rollout and who was required to complete the TDAC. He labeled the edit "removal of unnecessary self promotion."[^25]

These were not links to Nickles's own websites or commercial services. They pointed to other questions on Stack Exchange itself. Together with the edits at 17:09, 17:12, and 17:13, the revision histories document five consecutive edits targeting Nickles's TDAC content, repeatedly removing internal links under accusations of self-promotion.

At 17:12, Alsop edited the locked scam-warning question, replacing a link to another TDAC discussion with a direct link to Thailand's official immigration website. One minute later, he removed two more links to related Travel Stack Exchange questions from the warning's highest-scoring answer, again labeling the changes "removal of unnecessary self promotion."[^16][^18]

At 17:23, Alsop warned Nickles that his activity was "skirting the wrong side" of Stack Exchange's spam rules and that he risked being blocked.[^5] The revision records show that Alsop removed legitimate links between related travel questions and an official government fraud warning while accusing their author of self-promotion and spam.

### June 17–18: Arrival Card Tag Request

On June 17, 2025, Jesse Nickles proposed creating an `arrival-cards` tag on Travel Stack Exchange, explaining that digital arrival cards were becoming increasingly common worldwide and that related questions would benefit from a dedicated category.[^12]

When another contributor asked Nickles to identify existing questions suitable for the tag, Nickles expressed concern about receiving further warnings for linking his own content. He specifically wrote that he didn't want "to get warned again about linking to my own questions too much," and instead shared links to other relevant discussions.

The exchange illustrates a consequence of Alsop's earlier SEO accusations: Nickles had become reluctant to link related questions even when another community member explicitly requested examples. This was despite Stack Exchange's longstanding guidance recognizing the usefulness of linking related discussions.[^2]

### July 7: Independent Challenge to TDAC Duplicate Closure

On July 7, 2025, longtime Travel Stack Exchange contributor Franck Dernoncourt challenged the closure of Nickles's TDAC pricing question as a duplicate of the separate scam-warning discussion. "It's not a duplicate," Franck wrote, explaining that questions about official travel-document fees and questions about avoiding scams cover different subjects. He cited other visa-related discussions on Travel Stack Exchange as examples.[^1]

Nickles replied that moderators appeared concerned scammers might exploit TDAC discussions and acknowledged that this precaution could explain their decision. Franck then questioned why other questions about prices and scams were allowed to remain open if that was the justification.

No moderator publicly answered Franck's objection in that discussion, and the TDAC pricing question remained closed as a duplicate.[^1][^5]

## February 2026: Dispute Over Scam Website Links

On February 1, 2026, Travel Stack Exchange contributor Ryan Vasiya proposed that suspicious or fraudulent travel websites should be displayed as non-clickable text to prevent travelers from accidentally visiting them. He suggested using inline code formatting to identify potentially dangerous URLs without creating active hyperlinks.[^10]

Moderator Rory Alsop rejected the approach, stating: "Code highlight is for code - please do not use it for anything else." Despite the safety concern, Alsop objected to using code formatting to make suspicious domains non-clickable, without suggesting a consistent alternative for warning travelers about potentially fraudulent websites.

However, Travel Stack Exchange had already used inline code to display questionable website addresses for years. An October 2023 question about suspicious Korean K-ETA websites listed several competing domains as non-clickable text.[^11] When Vasiya pointed out this precedent, Alsop replied, "It's still not correct," and suggested he might edit that post as well.[^10]

Nickles believes suspected sockpuppet accounts associated with Chad Scira were exploiting this ambiguity by flagging warnings containing code-formatted domains as policy violations, attempting to confuse moderators and get legitimate scam warnings removed.

Other contributors supported safer ways to display suspicious domains, including replacing periods with `[.]` to prevent accidental clicks. The discussion highlighted how an established method of identifying potentially fraudulent websites had become a moderation dispute, with Alsop continuing to object to the formatting despite its previous use on Travel Stack Exchange.[^10]

## February 2026: Serial Downvoting Complaints

On February 1, 2026, Travel Stack Exchange contributor Ryan Vasiya reported that Stack Exchange had detected and reversed serial downvotes against his account. He also raised concerns about similar activity targeting other discussions involving Thailand.[^13] Stack Exchange's official guidance identifies serial downvoting as voting abuse and explains that its systems automatically reverse suspicious voting patterns.[^14]

Alsop was already familiar with these rules. In November 2013, he had personally directed another user reporting serial downvotes to Stack Exchange's canonical explanation of voting fraud.[^15]

In the February 2026 discussion, another contributor, Borsos Vidor, alleged that he had received a one-week suspension after reporting suspicious posting and flagging activity involving Thai visas, visa scams, and cannabis. He maintained that the problems were genuine and suggested that a visa agency might be involved.

On February 23, Alsop challenged Vidor's explanation for the suspension, stating that he had already been told the actual reason and adding, "do not make up lies, Boris." Alsop did not publicly explain the suspension reason or address Vidor's broader allegations about suspicious voting and flagging in that exchange.[^13]

Stack Exchange's published moderator guidelines say moderators should be "patient and fair," "lead by example," and show respect for community members in their words and actions.[^22] Its Moderator Agreement also requires moderators to follow the network-wide Code of Conduct.[^23]

Alsop's public accusation that a contributor reporting suspicious voting and flagging activity was lying stands in contrast to those expectations. Although Alsop claimed the contributor already knew why he had been suspended, he provided no explanation in the public discussion identifying what he believed was false.[^13]

## February 2026: Reopening Closed Questions

On February 13, 2026, Rory Alsop responded to a Travel Stack Exchange discussion about whether community members should help improve and reopen closed questions. He acknowledged that users might rescue a post containing a "good core question," but added that if its author had ignored site guidance or was "rude in comments," the "likelihood drops to near zero."[^20]

The statement is relevant to Nickles's June 2025 TDAC dispute, when he repeatedly requested an explanation for why his question about the official arrival-card price had been closed as a duplicate. Rather than addressing the distinction between pricing information and scam warnings, Alsop accused Nickles of SEO manipulation and warned him about spam.[^1][^5]

Travel Stack Exchange's published guidance bases reopening decisions on whether a question meets the site's requirements, whether the original closure reasons have been addressed, and whether users vote to reopen it.[^21] An author's perceived attitude toward moderators is not among those criteria.

## July 2026: Mass Deletion of Travel Questions

On July 21, 2026, Travel Stack Exchange contributor Franck Dernoncourt questioned why several useful travel questions had been deleted, including discussions about Thailand's Destination Thailand Visa (DTV), Hua Hin Airport, and travel to Vietnam.[^3]

Moderator **JonathanReez** responded that moderators and Stack Exchange administrators suspected the content originated from a particular individual's alleged sockpuppet accounts. He stated that they would "rather just nuke their content from orbit" than evaluate individual posts for accuracy or usefulness, describing the approach as a "scorched Earth" campaign with "no ifs, no buts."

When community members suggested preserving valuable questions and answers, JonathanReez rejected the proposal, insisting that suspected contributions would be removed regardless of their content. He suggested that other users could recreate deleted questions instead.

Stack Exchange's published rules explicitly permit individuals to maintain multiple accounts, provided they are not used for voting manipulation, circumventing suspensions, or evading other restrictions.[^7] Its deletion guidelines generally focus on removing spam, abusive material, and posts that fail the site's content requirements.[^8]

A longstanding Meta Stack Exchange discussion also supports preserving legitimate questions even when their authors have engaged in misconduct, with one moderator explaining that he deliberately retained useful questions while deleting problematic accounts.[^9]

Although Stack Exchange permits moderators to take action against abusive accounts, JonathanReez's publicly announced policy of deleting suspected contributions regardless of their individual merit raises questions about how these moderation decisions were justified and whether valuable travel information was unnecessarily removed.
